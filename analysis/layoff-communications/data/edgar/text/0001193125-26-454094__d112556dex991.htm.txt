OVERVIEW OF THE COMPANY’S BUSINESS OPERATIONS The Debtors, together with their Non-Debtor Affiliates, are a leading group of global forest products companies that process sustainably sourced wood into a wide range of products used primarily in paper, packaging, and construction applications. The Company’s principal executive office is in Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada and it has significant operations and assets in the United States, Canada, and Germany. Mercer’s shares of common stock are quoted and listed for trading on the NASDAQ Global Select Market under the symbol “MERC”. As described in greater detail below, the Company has two primary operating segments: the pulp segment (the “ Pulp Segment ”) and the solid wood segment (the “ Solid Wood Segment ”). Mercer is one of the world’s largest producers of “market” pulp and a growing leader in solid wood products. The Company’s large-scale, modern pulp mills produce northern bleached softwood kraft (“ NBSK ”) pulp, northern bleached hardwood kraft (“ NBHK ”) pulp, and biomass-based green electricity and bioextractives. Mercer also operates one of the world’s largest softwood lumber facilities and is firmly establishing itself as one of North America’s leading mass timber providers. In addition to traditional forest products, which are the core of its business, the Company generates green electricity using biofuels in connection with its pulping and sawmilling operations and sells surplus green electricity to third-party utilities and into regional electricity markets, thus providing the Company with a stable supplemental revenue source unrelated to pulp or lumber prices. 8 The following chart provides an overview of the Company’s global operations and geographic footprint: A. History and Formation Mercer was originally organized in 1968 as a business trust under the laws of the State of Washington. On March 1, 2006, Mercer converted from a business trust to a corporation organized under the laws of the State of Washington through a series of merger transactions. The Company’s principal executive offices are located in Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada. Mercer is the ultimate parent company of the Mercer International group and is the issuer of the Company’s publicly traded common stock and the Senior Notes. The Company’s operations are primarily carried out through Mercer’s various Non-Debtor Affiliates in the United States, Germany, and Canada. Mercer originally invested in various real estate assets with the intention of becoming a real estate investment trust. However, in 1985, the Company changed its strategic direction and began acquiring controlling interests in operating companies. In 1994, the Company entered the pulp business through the acquisition of the Rosenthal mill in Germany. Over the following decades, the Company expanded its pulp operations through a combination of organic investment and strategic acquisitions. In 1999, the Company converted the Rosenthal mill to the production of kraft pulp. The Company continued to expand its pulp operations with the construction of the Stendal mill in Germany, which commenced operations in 2004 following an investment of approximately $1.1 billion, and the subsequent acquisitions of the Celgar mill in British Columbia, Canada for approximately $210.0 million in 2005 and the Peace River mill in Alberta, Canada in 2018, as part of a share purchase transaction valued at approximately $344.0 million. Beginning in 2017, the Company expanded beyond its traditional pulp business into solid wood products with the acquisition of the Friesau mill in Germany. The Company subsequently expanded its solid wood business through the acquisition of the Spokane facility for approximately $51.3 million in 2021, the Torgau facility (through a share purchase transaction) for approximately $263.2 million in 2022, and the Conway facility and Okanagan facility from the Structurlam group of companies for approximately $82.1 million in 2023. 9 Throughout its history, the Company has pursued long-term growth in the forest products industry through a combination of strategic acquisitions and organic investments, including targeted capital expenditures to enhance and expand its existing assets. B. Current Business Operations i. Products & Services As described above, the Company’s two primary operating segments are the Pulp Segment and the Solid Wood Segment. Each segment is described in greater detail below. The following chart sets out the Company’s principal product and geographic sales mix for the six months ending June 30, 2026: (a) Pulp Segment The Pulp Segment consists of the manufacture, sale, and distribution of pulp, green electricity, and bio-chemicals at Mercer’s pulp mills located in Germany and Canada. The Company is one of the world’s largest producers of market NBSK pulp, a premium grade of pulp valued in part for its strength characteristics, which is sold primarily to tissue and paper producers. The Company operates four pulp mills, including two NBSK mills in Germany and two mills in Western Canada (one NBSK mill and one “swing” kraft mill capable of producing both NBSK and NBHK pulp). In addition to producing pulp, Mercer’s mills operate as large-scale bio-refineries that generate green electricity from biofuels produced during the pulp manufacturing process, including black liquor and wood waste. This energy is used to support mill operations, and surplus green electricity is sold to third-party utilities or into regional electricity markets. (b) Solid Wood Segment The Solid Wood Segment consists of the manufacture, sale, and distribution of lumber, engineered wood products, wood pallets, green electricity, biofuels, and wood residuals from the Company’s sawmills in Germany and its mass timber facilities in North America. The Company’s lumber products include dimensional, specialty, and other finished sawn lumber and are sold primarily to national and regional 10 distributors, construction firms, secondary manufacturers, retail yards, and home centers in the United States, Europe, and Asia. The Solid Wood Segment also involves the production of wood pallets, including Euro-pallets for customers primarily located in Europe. In addition, the Company manufactures engineered wood products, including cross-laminated timber (“ CLT ”), glulam, and finger joint lumber. CLT and glulam are used in residential and commercial construction, with CLT serving as a more sustainable alternative to traditional building materials, such as steel and concrete. Wood residuals generated through the manufacturing process are used internally, sold to third parties, or converted into products such as wood pellets and briquettes, while certain facilities also generate renewable electricity from biomass. ii. KEY EVENTS LEADING TO COMMENCEMENT OF CHAPTER 11 CASES A. Challenges Facing the Company ’ s Business Although the Company remains a leading global forest products producer, the Debtors are confronting acute financial challenges due to a confluence of factors, including among others: (i) an unsustainable capital structure driven by funding to support capacity expansion initiatives, (ii) significant near-term debt maturities, which would be challenging to refinance or extend absent the Restructuring, (iii) ongoing geopolitical developments, including evolving global trade and tariff dynamics, (iv) sluggish global economic growth, and (v) financial challenges, driven by substantial margin compression driven by rising input costs and adverse market conditions, including lower market prices for pulp. Each of these factors is described in greater detail below. Despite the Company’s recent efforts to address these challenges, including through negotiations with the lenders under the Canadian ABL Facility and the German RCF, cost-saving measures, liquidity-preservation initiatives, and other actions, the Company ultimately determined that a comprehensive financial restructuring through the Chapter 11 Cases was necessary to reduce its funded debt burden and associated interest expense, enhance liquidity, strengthen its balance sheet, address significant near-term debt maturities, and position the Company for greater financial stability on a go-forward basis. i. Capacity Expansion Initiatives and Increased Debt Financing In recent years, the Company has made substantial investments to increase its production capacity and broaden its product offerings. The Company began expanding beyond its traditional pulp business before the COVID-19 pandemic and accelerated that strategy beginning in 2021, particularly through investments in solid wood and mass timber. This period began amid strong demand and pricing in the U.S. and European lumber markets, including strong demand from the U.S. housing and home renovation markets and steady demand in Europe. From 2021 through 2023 alone, Mercer invested approximately $396.6 million in acquisitions to expand its solid wood operations and product mix. While these investments expanded the scale and breadth of the Company’s operations, they also required substantial capital and contributed to increased leverage and financial obligations, placing additional strain on the Company’s liquidity and overall financial position. • Friesau Mill . In April 2017, Mercer entered the solid wood business through its acquisition of the Friesau mill in Germany for approximately $61.6 million. The Friesau mill is a large-scale sawmill that produces lumber for customers in Europe, the United States, and other international markets. The acquisition represented a significant expansion of Mercer’s business beyond its traditional pulp operations. • Peace River Mill . Macroeconomic and Geopolitical Factors Mercer operates in highly cyclical industries in which the prices of pulp, lumber, and other forest products are driven largely by global supply and demand. In recent periods, continued economic uncertainty and weak conditions in key markets, elevated interest rates, and geopolitical uncertainty have contributed to challenging demand conditions for many of the Company’s products. At the same time, Mercer has 19 experienced significant increases in wood fiber costs and higher transportation costs, together with adverse foreign exchange effects and other cost pressures. The combination of weak demand, lower NBSK pulp pricing, elevated interest rates and input costs, and adverse foreign exchange movements has contributed to significant margin compression and placed pressure on the Company’s earnings, cash flows, and debt service capacity. • Weaker Demand and Pulp Pricing . Market conditions for pulp have remained challenging, particularly for NBSK pulp in China and North America. Demand in China, one of the world’s largest pulp markets, has been constrained by an oversupplied paper market and broader economic and trade uncertainty. During the first half of 2026, quoted NBSK net prices in China averaged approximately $672 per tonne, compared with approximately $764 per tonne during the same period in 2025, while quoted NBSK list prices in North America also declined materially. Mercer’s average NBSK sales realizations decreased approximately 11% during the first half of 2026 compared with the prior-year period. Demand for solid wood products in Europe has also remained subdued due to weak economic conditions. Although Solid Wood Segment revenues increased in the first half of 2026 and average lumber sales realizations increased by approximately 5%, lumber revenues declined due to lower sales volumes, and higher fiber costs more than offset the benefit of higher sales realizations, placing additional pressure on segment profitability. In the Pulp Segment, weaker demand has reduced the prices Mercer receives for its NBSK pulp at the same time that the Company is facing elevated production costs, placing significant pressure on margins and cash flows. • Higher Fiber and Other Input Costs . The Company has also experienced substantial increases in the cost of wood fiber, one of its most significant production inputs. These pressures have been particularly acute in Germany, where reduced fiber availability and increased demand for wood as an energy source, including in connection with ongoing geopolitical conflicts, have contributed to higher fiber costs. During the first half of 2026, Mercer’s per-unit pulp fiber costs increased approximately 18% compared with the same period in 2025, primarily due to reduced supply in Germany and Canada and strong demand for wood as an energy source in Germany. Fiber costs represented approximately 85% of lumber cash production costs during the first half of 2026, and per-unit fiber costs for lumber production increased approximately 32% year over year. High fiber costs also caused the Company to reduce production at its German pulp mills. As a result, the Company has faced higher production costs and, in certain cases, reduced production volumes. • Geopolitical and Energy Cost Pressures. Ongoing geopolitical conflicts, including the conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East, have increased economic uncertainty and have generally disrupted global energy markets. The conflict in the Middle East, in particular, has driven oil and other energy prices higher, adding inflationary pressure to freight, fiber, and other production costs. At the same time, heightened geopolitical uncertainty has weighed on macroeconomic activity, contributing to weaker demand for the Company’s products. These conditions have contributed to higher input costs at a time of weaker demand and pricing, thus putting further pressure on margins and cash flows. • Foreign Exchange Volatility . Mercer’s global geographic footprint also exposes the Company to foreign exchange risk. Most of the operating costs of the Company’s German mills are incurred in euros and most of the operating costs of its Canadian mills are incurred in Canadian dollars, while the majority of the Company’s products are priced in U.S. dollars. As a result, a weakening U.S. dollar relative to the euro or Canadian dollar can increase the Company’s costs in U.S. dollar terms and place pressure on operating margins and cash flows. During the first half of 2026, Mercer experienced an approximately $15.7 million negative foreign exchange impact on operating loss compared with the same period in 2025, primarily as a result of the weaker U.S. dollar increasing its euro- and Canadian-dollar-denominated costs and expenses. 20 Collectively, these conditions have contributed to weaker demand across the Company’s key markets and significant margin compression notwithstanding the Company’s continued efforts to reduce costs and improve operational efficiency. Mercer’s Operating EBITDA declined from approximately $243.7 million in 2024 to negative $22.2 million in 2025 and was negative $13.1 million during the first half of 2026, compared with positive $26.5 million during the same period in 2025. During the first half of 2026, the Company also incurred approximately $51.0 million of inventory impairment charges, primarily against pulp and fiber inventory as a result of low pulp prices and high fiber costs. The Company also used approximately $100.8 million of cash in operating activities during the first half of 2026, compared with approximately $7.5 million during the same period in 2025. Against the backdrop of the Company’s increased indebtedness described above, these macroeconomic and geopolitical factors have placed additional strain on the Company’s capital structure and liquidity. iv. Restricted Securities Section 1145(b) of the Bankruptcy Code defines “underwriter” for purposes of the Securities Act as one who, except with respect to ordinary trading transactions, (i) purchases a claim with a view to distribution of any security to be received in exchange for the claim, (ii) offers to sell securities issued under a plan for the holder of such securities, (iii) offers to buy securities issued under a plan from persons receiving such securities, if the offer to buy is made with a view to distribution, or (iv) is an issuer, as used in section 2(a)(11) of the Securities Act, with respect to such securities, which includes control persons of the issuer. The definition of an “issuer” for purposes of whether a person is an underwriter under section 1145(b)(1)(D) of the Bankruptcy Code includes Persons directly or indirectly controlling, controlled by or under direct or indirect common control with the issuer. “Control,” as defined in Rule 405 of the Securities Act, means the possession, directly or indirectly, of the power to direct or cause the direction of the management and policies of a Person, whether through the ownership of voting securities, by contract, or otherwise. The legislative history of section 1145 of the Bankruptcy Code suggests that a creditor who owns ten percent (10%) or more of a class of voting securities of a reorganized debtor may be presumed to be a “controlling person” and, therefore, an underwriter. However, the staff of the SEC has not endorsed this view. Notwithstanding the foregoing, “controlling person” underwriters may be able to sell securities without registration subject to the resale limitations of Rule 144 of the Securities Act, which permits the resale of securities received by such underwriters pursuant to a chapter 11 plan, subject to applicable volume limitations, notice and manner of sale requirements, and certain other conditions. Parties who believe they may be statutory underwriters as defined in section 1145 of the Bankruptcy Code or “affiliates” as defined in Rule 405 under the Securities Act are advised to consult with their own legal advisors as to the availability of the exemption provided by Rule 144. Rule 144 of the Securities Act defines an affiliate of the issuer as “a person that directly, or indirectly through one or more intermediaries, controls, or is controlled by, or is under common control with, such issuer.” A non-affiliate of an issuer that is not subject to the reporting requirements of section 13 or 15(d) of the Exchange Act and who has not been an affiliate of the issuer during the ninety days preceding such sale may resell securities after a one-year holding period whether or not there is current public information regarding the issuer, or six months if the issuer satisfies the current public information requirement under the Securities Act. An affiliate of an issuer that is not subject to the reporting requirements of section 13 or 15(d) of the Exchange Act may resell restricted securities after the one-year holding period, if applicable, if at the time of the sale certain current public information regarding the issuer is available, or after six months if the issuer satisfies the current public information requirement under the Securities Act. An affiliate must also comply with certain volume limitations, manner of sale requirements and certain other conditions of Rule 144 of the Securities Act. An affiliate of an issuer that receives securities under section 1145 of the Bankruptcy Code will not be subject to any holding period under Rule 144 of the Securities Act but will otherwise be subject to certain volume limitations, manner of sale requirements and certain other conditions of Rule 144 of the Securities Act. Cancellation of Debt In general, absent an exception, a debtor will realize and recognize cancellation of debt (“ COD ”) income upon satisfaction of its outstanding indebtedness for total consideration less than the amount of such indebtedness. The amount of COD income, in general, is the excess of (a) the adjusted issue price of the indebtedness satisfied over (b) the sum of (i) any amount of cash paid, (ii) the issue price of any debt issued, and (iii) the fair market value of any other consideration (including equity of a debtor or a party related to such debtor) given, in each case, in satisfaction, or as part of the discharge, of such indebtedness at the time of the exchange. However, COD income should not arise to the extent that payment of the indebtedness would have given rise to a deduction. The Plan provides that certain Holders of Claims or Interests will receive, among other things, New Common Stock, New Convertible Preferred Stock, Takeback Senior Secured Notes and Put Option Premium in exchange for their Claims or Interests, so the amount of COD income for the Mercer Group will depend in part on the fair market value of the New Common Stock and New Convertible Preferred Stock and on the issue price of the Takeback Senior Secured Notes and Put Option Premium on the Effective Date. The Put Option Premium, to the extent the Plan is consummated, is currently expected to be treated for U.S. federal income tax purposes as additional consideration received 31 by each Backstop Party in connection with the exchange of the Senior Notes. In addition, as discussed below, the receipt of the Subscription Rights to purchase the New Money Senior Secured Notes and to receive the Stapled Equity upon such purchase by the Eligible Holders of Allowed Senior Notes Claims may be treated as additional value received as a recovery on such Holders’ Claims. Accordingly, the estimated amount of COD income is uncertain at this time. Under section 108 of the Tax Code, a taxpayer is not required to include COD in gross income (i) if the debtor is under the jurisdiction of a court in a case under chapter 11 of the Bankruptcy Code and the discharge of debt occurs pursuant to that proceeding or (ii) to the extent that the taxpayer is insolvent immediately before the discharge. As a consequence of such an exclusion, a debtor generally must reduce its tax attributes by the amount of COD income that it excluded from gross income. In general, such tax attributes are reduced in the following order: (a) NOLs; (b) general business credit carryovers; (c) minimum tax credit carryovers; (d) capital loss carryovers; (e) tax basis in assets (but not below the amount of liabilities to which the taxpayer remains subject immediately after the cancellation of indebtedness); (f) passive activity loss and credit carryovers; and (g) foreign tax credit carryovers. Alternatively, a taxpayer with excluded COD may elect first to reduce the tax basis of its depreciable assets (a “ Section 108(b)(5) Election ”), in which case the limitation described in (e) does not apply to the reduction in tax basis of depreciable property and, following such reduction, any remaining COD income that is excluded from gross income reduces any remaining tax attributes in the order specified in the prior sentence. The reduction of the taxpayer’s tax attributes occurs at the end of the taxable year for which the excluded COD income is realized, but only after the taxpayer’s net income or loss for the taxable year of the debt discharge has been determined; in this way, the attribute reduction is generally effective as of the start of the taxable year following the discharge. If the amount of excluded COD income exceeds available tax attributes, the excess generally is not subject to U.S. federal income tax. Where a taxpayer joins in the filing of a consolidated U.S. federal income tax return, applicable Treasury Regulations require, in certain circumstances, that certain tax attributes of other members of the group also be reduced. In connection with the implementation of the Plan, the Debtors expect to realize a substantial amount of excluded COD income for U.S. federal income tax purposes. The Debtors currently do not intend to make a Section 108(b)(5) Election and expect that substantially all of their NOLs and a portion of their stock and asset tax basis will be reduced as a result of the COD attribute reduction arising in connection with the Plan. 2. General Annual Limitation In general, the amount of the annual limitation to which a corporation or consolidated group that undergoes an ownership change would be subject is equal to the product of (a) the fair market value of the stock of the corporation (or parent of the consolidated group) immediately before the ownership change (with certain adjustments) and (b) the “long-term tax-exempt rate” (which is the highest of the adjusted federal long-term rates in effect for any month in the three (3)-calendar-month period ending with the calendar month in which the ownership change occurs, e.g. , 4.18% for ownership changes occurring in November 2026). For a corporation or consolidated group in bankruptcy that undergoes an ownership change pursuant to a confirmed bankruptcy plan, unless the special bankruptcy exception described below applies, the annual limitation is generally determined by reference to the fair market value of the stock of the corporation (or the parent of the consolidated group) immediately after (rather than before) the ownership change and after giving effect to the discharge of creditors’ claims, subject to certain adjustments; in no event, however, can the stock value for this purpose exceed the pre-change gross value of the assets of the corporation. Any portion of the annual limitation that is not used in a given year may be carried forward, thereby adding to the annual limitation for the subsequent taxable year. Under certain circumstances, the annual limitation otherwise computed may be increased if the corporation or consolidated group has an overall built-in gain in its assets at the time of the ownership change. If a “loss corporation” (as defined in section 382(k)(1) of the Tax Code) or consolidated group has such “net unrealized built-in gain” (“ NUBIG ”) at the time of an ownership change (taking into account most assets and items of “built-in” income, gain, loss, and deduction), any built-in gains recognized (or, according to IRS Notice 2003-65 (as modified by IRS Notice 2018-30)), treated as recognized) during the following sixty (60)-month period (up to the amount of the original NUBIG) generally will increase the annual limitation in the year of such recognition, such that the loss corporation or consolidated group would be permitted to use its pre-change losses against such built-in gain income in addition to its otherwise applicable annual limitation. Alternatively, if a loss corporation or consolidated group has a “net unrealized built-in loss” (“ NUBIL ”) at the time of an ownership change, then any built-in losses existing at such time that are recognized (including, but not limited to, amortization or depreciation deductions attributable to such built-in losses) during the sixty (60)-month period following the ownership change (up to the amount of the original NUBIL) will be treated as pre-change losses, the deductibility of which will be subject to the annual limitation. In general, the NUBIG or NUBIL will be deemed to be zero unless it is greater than the lesser of (a) $10 million or (b) fifteen percent (15%) of the fair market value of the corporation’s or consolidated group’s assets (with certain adjustments) before the ownership change. The Debtors have not determined at this time whether the Mercer Group will have a NUBIG or a NUBIL as of the Effective Date. If the corporation or consolidated group does not continue its historic business or use a significant portion of its historic assets in a new business for at least two (2) years after the ownership change, the annual limitation resulting from the ownership change is reduced to zero, thereby precluding any utilization of the corporation’s or consolidated group’s pre-change losses (subject to potential increase for recognized built-in gains in the manner described above if the corporation is in a NUBIG position at the time of the ownership change). Under applicable Treasury Regulations, the modification of the terms of a debt instrument (including pursuant to an actual exchange of a new debt instrument for the existing debt instrument) generally is a significant modification if, based on all of the facts and circumstances and taking into account all modifications of the debt instrument, the legal rights or obligations that are altered and the degree to which they are altered is “economically significant.” In addition to the general rule, the Treasury Regulations provide specific rules under which certain modifications will be treated as significant modifications. A modification that changes the timing of payments due under a debt instrument is a significant modification if it results in a material deferral of scheduled payments. The determination of whether a particular debt obligation constitutes a “security” depends on an overall evaluation of the nature of the debt, including whether the holder of such debt obligation is subject to a material level of entrepreneurial risk and whether a continuing proprietary interest is intended or not. Character of Gain or Loss Where gain or loss is recognized by a U.S. Holder in an exchange of Allowed Claims pursuant to the Plan, the character of such gain or loss as long-term or short-term capital gain or loss or as ordinary income or loss will be determined by a number of factors, including the tax status of such U.S. Holder, whether the Claims constitute capital assets in the hands of such U.S. Holder and how long they have been held, whether the Claims were acquired at a market discount, and whether and to what extent the Holder previously claimed a worthlessness deduction with respect to the Claims. In general, any gain or loss should be long-term capital gain or loss if the U.S. Holder held the Claims, as applicable, as capital assets and such U.S. Holder’s holding period in the Claims is more than one (1) year at the time of the relevant exchange. A reduced tax rate on long-term capital gain may apply to non-corporate U.S. Holders. The deductibility of capital losses is subject to limitations. A U.S. Holder that purchased its Senior Notes Claims from a prior holder at a “market discount” (relative to the principal amount of the Senior Notes Claims at the time of acquisition) may be subject to the market discount rules of the Tax Code. In general, a debt instrument is considered to have been acquired with market discount if the holder’s adjusted tax basis in the debt instrument is less than its “stated redemption price at maturity” (which generally would be equal to the stated principal amount if all stated interest was required to be paid in cash or property at least annually). Under these rules, any gain recognized on the exchange of the Senior Notes Claims (which, as discussed below, does not include amounts received in respect of accrued but unpaid interest) generally will be treated as ordinary income to the extent of the market discount accrued (on a straight line basis or, at the election of the U.S. Holder, on a constant yield basis) during the U.S. Holder’s period of ownership, unless such U.S. Holder elected to include the market discount in income as it accrued. If a U.S. Holder of the Senior Notes Claims did not elect to include market discount in income as it accrued and, thus, under the market discount rules, was required to defer all or a portion of any deductions for interest on debt incurred or maintained to purchase or carry its Senior Notes Claims, such deferred amounts should become deductible at the time of the exchange to the extent the exchange is a taxable disposition. To the extent that the Senior Notes Claims acquired with a market discount are exchanged in a tax-deferred transaction, any market discount that accrued on such Senior Notes Claims but was not recognized by the U.S. Holder generally is carried over to the Offered Instruments received in exchange for the Senior Notes Claims the tax basis of which is determined in whole or in part by reference to the U.S. Holder’s tax basis in the Senior Notes Claims (where such Offered Instruments received may include the New Common Stock and the New Convertible Preferred Stock, such that any gain recognized on a subsequent disposition of such Offered Instruments generally could be treated as ordinary income to the extent of such carried-over accrued market discount, and in such case a U.S. Holder may also be treated as having market discount on 39 the New Senior Secured Notes received (if treated as “securities”), (in addition to, and without duplication of, any accrued market discount described above) to the extent such holder’s initial tax basis in the New Senior Secured Notes is less than their issue price by more than a de minimis amount. U.S. Holders who acquired their Senior Notes Claims other than at original issuance should consult their tax advisors regarding the possible application of the market discount rules. 5. Issue Price Determination Holders of Allowed Senior Notes Claims are receiving the Subscription Rights, Takeback Senior Secured Notes, New Common Stock, and New Convertible Preferred Stock in exchange for their Claims, and Holders exercising their Subscription Rights are receiving additional New Money Senior Secured Notes and the Stapled Equity in exchange for the amount surrendered – i.e., the sum of the amount of cash paid upon exercise of the Subscription Rights and the Holder’s Subscription Rights received in the exchange of their Allowed Senior Notes Claims. Similarly, the Takeback Senior Secured Notes received together with the New Common Stock, the New Convertible Preferred Stock and the Subscription Rights in exchange for Allowed Senior Notes Claims may be treated as issued as part of an investment unit. Under the investment unit rules, the “issue price” of an investment unit consisting of a debt instrument and one or more other property rights (such as stock or an option) is first determined as if the investment unit were a debt instrument, and such issue price is then allocated between the debt instrument and the other property rights based on their relative fair market values. The portion so allocated to the debt instrument is its issue price. 40 Under applicable Treasury Regulations, if a substantial amount of the debt instruments in an issue is issued for cash, the issue price of the debt instrument is determined based on the cash price. Although not defined, a substantial amount of a debt offering could be ten percent or more of the offering. In the present case, the Investment Package will be acquired, pursuant to the Subscription Rights, for a combination of cash and the Holder’s Subscription Rights. Alternatively, in the case of an integrated transaction, the New Common Stock, New Convertible Preferred Stock and New Senior Secured Notes will be acquired for a combination of the U.S. Holder’s Senior Notes Claim and cash. Although not entirely clear, the Debtors believe that no portion of the New Senior Secured Notes was issued solely for cash. In the absence of a cash price, the issue price of a debt instrument depends on whether a substantial amount of the debt instruments or the property exchanged therefor are considered to be “traded on an established market” at any time during the 31-day period ending 15 days after the issue date. Pursuant to applicable Treasury Regulations, an “established market” need not be a formal market. It is sufficient if there is a readily available sales price for an executed purchase or sale of the debt instrument or the property exchanged therefor, or if there are one or more “firm quotes” or “indicative quotes” with respect to such instrument or property, as such terms are defined in applicable Treasury Regulations. If the debt instruments received or a substantial amount of the property exchanged are considered traded on an established market, the issue price of the debt instruments would for U.S. federal income tax purposes equal (or approximate) their fair market value as of the Effective Date. If only one component of an “investment unit” is considered traded on an established market, it is unclear whether the investment unit would be considered so traded. If there is no such substantial amount treated as sold for money and the investment unit is not considered “publicly traded,” but the Senior Notes are considered publicly traded, the issue price of the investment unit would be determined by reference to the fair market value of the Senior Notes as of the Effective Date. If neither the New Senior Secured Notes nor the investment unit is publicly traded, the issue price of the New Senior Secured Notes generally will be their stated principal amount. The amount of OID includible in income for a taxable year by a U.S. Holder of the New Senior Secured Notes generally will equal the sum of the “daily portions” of the total OID on the New Senior Secured Notes for each day during the taxable year (or portion thereof) on which such U.S. Holder held the New Senior Secured Notes. Generally, the daily portion of the OID is determined by allocating to each day during an accrual period a ratable portion of the OID on such New Senior Secured Notes that is allocable to the accrual period in which such day is included. The amount of OID allocable to each accrual period generally will be an amount equal to the product of the “adjusted issue price” of the New Senior Secured Notes at the beginning of such accrual period and its yield to maturity. The “adjusted issue price” of the New Senior Secured Notes at the beginning of any accrual period generally will equal the issue price, increased by the total OID accrued for each prior accrual period, less any cash payments made on such New Senior Secured Notes (other than payments of qualified stated interest) on or before the first day of the accrual period. Under the OID rules, the Debtors will be presumed to pay interest in cash with respect to each period for which a PIK option exists if the Debtors’ exercise of their option to pay PIK Interest would not result in a lower yield to maturity of the New Senior Secured Notes. Amortizable Bond Premium, Acquisition Premium The amount of OID includible in a U.S. Holder’s gross income with respect to the New Senior Secured Notes will be reduced or eliminated if the New Senior Secured Note is acquired (or deemed to be acquired) at a “premium.” A debt instrument is acquired at a “premium” if the holder’s tax basis in the debt is greater than the adjusted issue price of the debt at the time of the acquisition. If a U.S. Holder acquires any interest in the New Senior Secured Notes at a premium, the amount of any OID includible in its gross income in any taxable year with respect to the New Senior Secured Notes will be (i) eliminated if the U.S. Holder’s tax basis in the New Senior Secured Notes exceeds their stated redemption price at maturity (“amortizable bond premium”), or (ii) otherwise, reduced by an allocable portion of the premium (generally determined by multiplying the annual OID accrual with respect to the New Senior Secured Notes by a fraction, the numerator of which is the amount of the premium, and the denominator of which is the total OID) (“acquisition premium”). Alternatively, in the case of acquisition premium, if a holder is willing to treat all stated interest as OID (including qualified stated interest), such holder may elect to recompute the OID accruals by treating its acquisition as a purchase at original issue and applying the constant yield method. An election to amortize bond premium applies to all debt instruments held or subsequently acquired by the U.S. Holder and may not be revoked without the consent of the IRS. Redemption of New Convertible Preferred Stock—Distribution vs. Sale or Exchange Treatment A redemption of New Convertible Preferred Stock by Reorganized Mercer for cash will be a taxable transaction for U.S. federal income tax purposes. A U.S. Holder whose New Convertible Preferred Stock is redeemed will be treated, depending on such U.S. Holder’s particular circumstances, either as recognizing gain or loss from the sale or exchange of the redeemed New Convertible Preferred Stock or as receiving a distribution from Reorganized Mercer, as described in more detail below. Under the stock redemption rules of section 302 of the Tax Code, a U.S. Holder will recognize gain or loss on a redemption of New Convertible Preferred Stock for cash if the redemption (a) results in a “complete termination” of all such U.S. Holder’s equity interest in Reorganized Mercer, (b) results in a “substantially disproportionate” redemption with respect to such U.S. Holder, or (c) is “not essentially equivalent to a dividend” with respect to such U.S. Holder (together, the “ Section 302 Tests ”). In applying the Section 302 Tests, a U.S. Holder must take into account stock of Reorganized Mercer (including New Common Stock and New Convertible Preferred Stock) that such U.S. Holder actually owns and that such U.S. Holder constructively owns under certain attribution rules, pursuant to which the U.S. Holder will be treated as owning stock of Reorganized Mercer owned by certain family members (except that in the case of a “complete termination” a U.S. Holder may waive, under certain circumstances, attribution from family members) and related entities, and stock of Reorganized Mercer that the U.S. Holder has the right to acquire by exercise of an option (which, for this purpose, may include New Common Stock that such U.S. Holder has the right to acquire upon conversion of New Convertible Preferred Stock). A redemption of New Convertible Preferred Stock will be a substantially disproportionate redemption with respect to a U.S. Holder if (i) the percentage of the outstanding voting stock of Reorganized Mercer owned (directly and by attribution) by such U.S. Holder immediately after the redemption is less than 80% of the same percentage owned (directly and by attribution) by such U.S. Holder immediately before the redemption, (ii) the percentage of the outstanding common stock (voting or nonvoting) of Reorganized Mercer owned (directly and by attribution) by such U.S. Holder immediately after the redemption is less than 80% of the same percentage owned (directly and by attribution) by such U.S. Holder immediately before the redemption, and (iii) immediately after the redemption, such U.S. Holder owns (directly and by attribution) less than 50% of the total combined voting power of all classes of stock of Reorganized Mercer entitled to vote. If a redemption of New Convertible Preferred Stock fails to satisfy the “substantially disproportionate” test, the redemption nonetheless may satisfy the “not essentially equivalent to a dividend” test. A redemption of New Convertible Preferred Stock will generally satisfy the “not essentially equivalent to a dividend” test if it results in a “meaningful reduction” of the U.S. Holder’s equity interest in Reorganized Mercer. A redemption of New Convertible Preferred Stock that results in any reduction of the proportionate equity interest in Reorganized Mercer held by a U.S. Holder with a relative equity interest that is minimal and who does not exercise any control over or participate in the management of Reorganized Mercer should generally be treated as “not essentially equivalent to a dividend.” U.S. Holders are urged to consult their tax advisors regarding the application of the rules of section 302 of the Tax Code in their particular circumstances. 47 Reorganized Mercer cannot predict whether any particular U.S. Holder will be subject to sale or exchange treatment, on the one hand, or distribution treatment, on the other hand, upon a redemption of New Convertible Preferred Stock.
