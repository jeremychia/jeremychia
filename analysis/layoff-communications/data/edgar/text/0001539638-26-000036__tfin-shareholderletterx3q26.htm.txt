Given the foregoing, Triumph’s earnings will continue to have a strong correlation to the average invoice sizes we purchase from our Factoring clients. For 3Q, the average transportation invoice size in our Factoring business was $2,210. For the first 16 days of 4Q, average transportation invoice sizes have increased to $2,288. The four main inputs for trucking – wages, fuel, equipment costs, and insurance – are all continuing to trend upward. Fuel is up materially since the beginning of the Iran conflict. Investors sometimes ask how sensitive our average invoice price is to the cost of diesel. That question cannot be answered with precision because there are many forces at play setting the 2 market rate. For some of our larger fleet customers with shipper-direct contracts, the fuel surcharge per mile is running nearly $1.00, which is extraordinarily high by historical standards; however, that surcharge is only relative to the pricing scale when contract rates were set. For the average load, we estimate that fuel is roughly 20-25% of the cost, being second only to driver wages. For example, dry van revenue per mile is up ~40% YoY, but diesel costs are up 67% if you use the price at the pump, which is not what most truckers pay due to negotiated fuel discounts. Beyond that cost pressure, it is hard to find qualified drivers, which is pushing wages to $0.70 a mile and beyond in many markets. Insurance costs continue to increase, in part due to nuclear verdicts. The cost to purchase equipment has gone up, and the cost to finance it has also gone up as interest rates have risen. In sum, we cannot give a precise calculation for what diesel prices will do to the average invoice because it depends on too many factors, not the least of which is demand. What we can say is that all of the four major inputs listed above are currently inflationary. As a result, the revenue per mile a trucker must generate (and thus, the average invoice size) is almost certainly going to be double-digit percentages above historical norms unless and until market forces change. Personally, I am not convinced that current rates have reached a level where the average law-abiding trucking company can earn its true cost of capital. Averages do not tell the full story – carriers with longstanding customer relationships or highly efficient operations are doing considerably better in this market than they have in several years. Whatever the market does, here is a helpful calculation for investors: for every $100 move higher in the average invoice price, Triumph should generate roughly $12 million in incremental annual revenue, $8.5 million in pretax earnings and $0.27 EPS annualized . The inverse is also true when invoice sizes decline. Autonomous technology is coming. I have sat in many of our customers’ newer trucks and seen firsthand the advanced safety features already built around this technology. Our larger customers, which generate the majority of our Factoring revenue, are well positioned to adapt their fleets as the technology evolves, and we intend to help them do so. A Different Freight Cycle: Defensibility and Durability This freight environment is more favorable for Triumph than the market appreciates. Capacity exits, regulatory enforcement, driver shortages, and elevated operating costs continue to tighten truckload supply, supporting higher rates despite only moderate freight demand growth. Across the industry, carriers are reporting stronger bid activity, improving pricing, positive levels of tender rejections, and increasing customer urgency around securing committed capacity. Spot and contract rates remain well above prior-year levels, and most management teams in our space view recent softness as seasonal rather than cyclical deterioration. What makes this cycle unusual is that pricing is strengthening before demand. Recent shipper surveys show freight-volume expectations roughly flat, even as expectations for capacity tightness and pricing continue to rise. What we see in our own transaction data is that the constraint is on the supply side. The scarce resource in freight today is not trucks. It is trusted, deployable capacity, because a truck that cannot be verified and deployed with confidence is not defensible, and thus, is not truly usable capacity. Freight is moving from a market defined by abundant capacity to one defined by defensible capacity. The key question is no longer whether a truck is available, but whether that capacity can be trusted, verified, insured, financially capable, and thus deployed with confidence. While there may still be plenty of trucks on the road, the pool of carriers that brokers and shippers are willing and able to use is shrinking. As a result, the economics of freight are changing and suggest the market is placing a growing premium on commercially usable capacity. In a market where capacity increasingly must be verified before it can be trusted, identity, transaction history, payment history and financial behavior become more valuable. Our transaction data suggests the market is already operating with less flexibility than headline capacity metrics imply. We see typical seasonal volume patterns against a known constraint in capacity. Our data suggests that the industry has substantially less capacity available to absorb a recovery in demand than traditional headline measures would imply. Broker buy rates remain elevated across all major equipment types, rising roughly 48% YoY in September, even as freight demand remains relatively stable. Historically, that type of pricing inflation would have required a significant demand surge. Today, it is occurring before one arrives. 8 The implication is that freight capacity appears increasingly constrained on the supply side. Regulatory enforcement, carrier qualification standards, insurance requirements, fraud mitigation efforts, and driver compliance and training requirements are all raising the threshold for what capacity can participate in the market. We believe those forces are unlikely to reverse in the near term and may continue removing capacity from the system. As a result, our base case is that capacity replacement will occur more slowly than in prior trucking cycles. Before COVID, markets typically self-corrected as higher rates attracted drivers, equipment, and new entrants. Today, new capacity must also satisfy a broader set of compliance, safety, insurance, and documentation standards before it becomes commercially deployable. That creates a structural bottleneck that could extend well beyond the traditional freight cycle. The most important forward-looking risk is what happens if demand accelerates into this environment. Several macro indicators have begun to improve, and any meaningful recovery in housing, manufacturing, durable goods, or consumer activity would likely arrive against a capacity base that is already tightening. In that scenario, freight markets could experience sharper rate inflation than many participants currently expect because demand would be moving faster than replacement capacity can enter the market. 9 We are also watching the evolution of broker economics closely. Current transaction data shows that higher rates have not translated into proportionately higher margin percentages on an average load basis. However, total broker gross margins and market volumes have continued to improve, suggesting that brokers are increasingly being relied upon to solve capacity challenges as route guides become less reliable, or as shippers consolidate brokers. If capacity constraints persist, network scale, carrier relationships, and access to verified capacity should become increasingly valuable competitive advantages. Taken together, the data points toward a market that remains early in a supply-driven tightening cycle. Our view is that the industry’s defining question over the next 12 to 24 months will not be whether freight demand recovers; it will be whether defensible capacity can recover alongside it. Based on what we see in transaction-level activity, the answer is likely to be “not quickly,” creating the potential for a longer-lasting and more structurally supportive freight environment than market participants have experienced in prior recoveries. We acknowledge that this produces results that can be confusing without more context. Our mortgage warehouse deposit balances, which come from the servicing operations of our mortgage warehouse clients, averaged $2.6 billion during the quarter. These deposits are non-interest bearing deposits to the owner of the funds, so that is how we classify them on our balance sheet. We compensate our clients (the mortgage companies) for these deposits via an interest rebate on their loans – similarly to the way an earnings credit rate might be applied to treasury management fees for a traditional commercial deposit account. Historically, we recognized the full cost of these rebates as an offset to interest income. Those balances have grown materially so far this year, and for some clients the rebates now exceed the interest they pay us on their loans. When that happens, we record the rebate as an offset to interest income until that client’s interest paid to us is reduced to zero, and we record the excess as interest expense. Because the related deposit balances are technically not interest bearing, the interest expense we record inflates the calculated average cost of our interest bearing deposits. We show you the impact on deposit costs in the Mortgage Warehouse Excess Rebates schedule included with tables following this letter. We sell a portion of these deposits overnight to the Federal Reserve, and they enable us to reduce our use of wholesale funding. The total effective cost of these deposits to us is below the rate the Federal Reserve pays on our cash balances, resulting in very low-risk incremental net interest income. While we are happy with the growth we have experienced this year in our mortgage warehouse deposit business, our traditional community bank deposit franchise continues to provide the foundation of our funding base: roughly $3 billion of deposits with an average cost below 1.15% . Our community bank team deserves a tremendous amount of credit for serving our customers so well that we are able to maintain profitable, value-adding relationships through any rate cycle. The largest increase in non-performing loans came from a $38.4 million relationship comprised of 4 loans where unauthorized liens were placed behind Triumph. The relationship matured and was placed on non-accrual as we pursue resolution. Despite the maturity default, loan payments continue and we have not recognized a credit loss, reflecting our current view of the underlying collateral and expected repayment. Separately, two property-specific situations contributed to credit expense. In one case, an extraordinary event caused structural damage that led to a disruption in operations. The other involves a property generating positive operating income, but for which stabilized operations have been unable to service debt as originally structured. This resulted in a tax delinquency and forbearance agreement. We believe that the borrower has the means and intent to resolve the tax delinquency and is making payments in accordance with the forbearance agreement. Our 2027 earnings framework assumes credit costs continue to trend toward a normal level, but credit will never be perfectly linear. We will continue to narrow the fairway of community bank credit, resolve non-core exposures, and maintain the capital and liquidity necessary to absorb unforeseen outcomes should any arise. As stated last quarter, the metrics also reflect credit exposure work tied to some of the business lines we are exiting, as well as normal-course credit noise. We continue to monitor the bankruptcy proceedings related to our Tricolor ABL exposure, and we continue to believe that our loan position is adequately secured by inventory and other assets that serve as our collateral. Given their legal form, these deposits are classified as noninterest bearing deposits on our balance sheet. Despite their classification, many of these deposits are not truly free of cost as we compensate our clients for these balances in the form of an earnings interest rebate rather than deposit interest. As a result, such noninterest bearing deposits first decrease our loan yield before increasing our deposit rates. Because of the recent influx of these deposits, some clients hold significantly higher deposit balances than loan balances. As a result, we are now, in some cases, paying out more in rebates than we earn in interest on a given client’s loans. In those cases, we are recording that excess rebate as deposit interest expense. Whether recorded as interest expense or an offset to interest income, the effect on net interest income and net income is the same: these are profitable funds as they displace more expensive wholesale funding and can be invested risk-free above their cost.
