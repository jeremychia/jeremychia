The increase also reflected growth in revenue, mainly from banking net interest income (‘banking NII’) and lower expected credit losses and other credit impairment charges (‘ECL’). Profit after tax increased by $2.1bn or 38% to $7.6bn compared with 3Q25. - Constant currency profit before tax excluding notable items increased by $0.9bn to $10.0bn compared with 3Q25. - Annualised return on average tangible equity (‘RoTE‘) in 3Q26 was 17.7% compared with 12.3% in 3Q25. Excluding notable items, annualised RoTE in 3Q26 was 18.2%, a rise of 1.6 percentage points compared with 3Q25. - Revenue increased by $0.8bn to $18.6bn compared with 3Q25, primarily reflecting growth in banking NII. Fee and other income also increased in our Corporate and Institutional Banking (‘CIB’) business in Wholesale Transaction Banking (‘WTB’) and in Debt and Equity Markets, supported by higher customer activity, and in Investment Distribution and Private Bank in Wealth. This was partly offset by valuation losses in Corporate Centre and the non-recurrence of one-off favourable impacts in 3Q25 in Insurance. Constant currency revenue excluding notable items rose by $0.8bn to $18.7bn. - Net interest income (‘NII’) of $9.3bn increased by $0.5bn or 6% compared with 3Q25. The increase reflected deposit balance growth and the benefit of reinvestment of our structural hedge at higher yields, partly offset by lower NII from Markets Treasury and an adverse impact of $0.1bn from one-off items in 3Q26. Banking NII increased by $0.6bn to $11.7bn. Banking NII excludes insurance NII, which fell by $0.1bn, and the funding costs associated with the trading book, which remained broadly stable. - Net interest margin (‘NIM’) of 1.57% was stable compared with 3Q25. - ECL of $0.8bn decreased by $0.2bn or 17% compared with 3Q25, primarily reflecting a $0.1bn reduction in stage 3 charges related to the Hong Kong commercial real estate (‘CRE’) sector, as well as lower charges in our UK business and International Wealth and Premier Banking (‘IWPB’) business in Mexico. - Operating expenses of $8.8bn were $1.3bn or 12% lower compared with 3Q25. This included a favourable net impact of $1.4bn from notable items, which reflected the non-recurrence of legal provisions in 3Q25. Excluding notable items, the increase in operating expenses primarily reflected the impact of inflation, higher planned spend and investment in technology, and the phasing of the performance-related pay accrual relative to 3Q25, partly mitigated by the cost reduction benefits from our organisational simplification. Target basis operating expenses were $0.1bn or 1.4% higher than in 3Q25, including the impact of inflation and higher planned spend and investment in technology, partly offset by the cost reduction benefits from our organisational simplification. - Customer lending balances increased by $7.8bn compared with 2Q26 on a reported basis. On a constant currency basis, lending balances increased by $6.7bn. This primarily reflected growth as we supported our customers to participate in the India Foreign Currency Non-Resident Deposit scheme, together with growth in our UK and Hong Kong businesses. This was partly offset by the classification to held for sale of lending balances associated with the exit of our retail banking business in Australia in our IWPB business. - Customer accounts increased by $9.1bn compared with 2Q26 on a reported basis. On a constant currency basis, customer accounts increased by $6.5bn, reflecting continued momentum in deposit inflows in our Hong Kong business. In our IWPB business, deposit growth was mainly in India, as we facilitated customer participation in the India Foreign Currency Non-Resident Deposit scheme. This growth was partly offset by retail deposit outflows following our announced exit of our retail banking business in Australia. In CIB, lower deposit balances reflected outflows of short-term balances. - Common equity tier 1 (‘CET1’) capital ratio of 13.9% decreased by 0.2 percentage points compared with 30 June 2026, reflecting an increase in risk-weighted assets (‘RWAs‘), together with a decrease in the fair value of hold-to-collect-and-sell debt instruments due to higher yields and the impact of the share buy-back announced at our interim results, partly offset by regulatory profits net of dividends. - The Board has approved a third interim dividend of $0.10 per share. On 28 September 2026, we completed the $1bn share buy-back announced at our interim results. We also intend to initiate a share buy-back of up to $1bn, which we expect to complete by our 2026 full-year results announcement. About HSBC HSBC Holdings plc, the parent company of HSBC, is headquartered in London. With assets of $3.4tn at 30 September 2026, HSBC is one of the world’s largest banking and financial services organisations. The Group‘s operating segments comprise of four businesses along with Corporate Centre: - Hong Kong - UK - Corporate and Institutional Banking - International Wealth and Premier Banking Our Hong Kong business comprises Retail Banking and Wealth and Commercial Banking of HSBC Hong Kong and Hang Seng Bank Limited (‘Hang Seng Bank‘). Our UK business comprises UK Retail Banking and Wealth (including first direct and M&S Bank) and UK Commercial Banking, including HSBC Innovation Bank. CIB integrates our Commercial Banking business (outside of the UK and Hong Kong) with our Global Banking and Markets business. IWPB comprises Premier banking outside of Hong Kong and the UK, our Private Bank, Asset Management and Insurance businesses. Corporate Centre results primarily comprise the financial impact from certain acquisitions and disposals and the share of profit, dilution and impairment loss impacts from interests in our associates and joint ventures. It also includes Central Treasury, stewardship costs and consolidation adjustments. Reshaping the Group for growth Privatisation of Hang Seng Bank On 26 January 2026, we completed our privatisation of Hang Seng Bank, following shareholder and Court approval. Hang Seng Bank is now a wholly-owned subsidiary of the HSBC Group and Hang Seng Bank shares have been withdrawn from the Hong Kong Stock Exchange. Through the privatisation of Hang Seng Bank, we expect to realise $0.5bn in annualised pre-tax revenue and cost synergies across both our brands in Hong Kong by the end of 2028. We expect to incur associated restructuring costs of $0.6bn, of which one-off income statement impacts would be reported as material notable items. We also have an ambition to generate further annualised revenue and cost opportunities of around $0.4bn by the end of 2028 across both our brands in Hong Kong. Organisational simplification At our 2024 full-year results we announced measures to simplify the Group, committing to deliver an annualised reduction of around $1.5bn in our cost base by the end of 2026, spending $1.8bn in restructuring and other related costs. At our 1H26 interim results, having already exceeded our savings commitment of $1.5bn, we revised our simplification savings commitment with an intention to action around $2bn of annualised savings by the end of 2026, spending up to our originally expected $1.8bn of restructuring costs. The additional saves we have brought into the programme generate capacity that will be used to support the growth of our four businesses. Since the start of 2025, we have identified and actioned annualised cost savings of approximately $1.9bn, spending associated restructuring and other related costs of $1.6bn. These actions have resulted in a reduction of around $0.6bn in operating expenses in the income statement in 2025, and a $0.8bn reduction in 9M26. Strategic transactions We remain focused on opportunities where we have a clear competitive advantage and accretive returns, and we aim to redeploy approximately $1.8bn of additional costs saved from non-strategic activities into these areas over the medium term. This includes the additional $0.3bn of costs saved from the synergies generated from our privatisation of Hang Seng Bank. During the third quarter of 2026, we completed the sale of our operations in Uruguay. In addition, we announced the sale of HSBC Life Singapore. In Australia, we announced the sale of our portfolio of home and personal loans, the planned wind-down of the remaining retail business and the transfer of our CIB, Private Banking and Asset Management businesses to the Sydney branch of The Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation Limited, simplifying our entity footprint. In Egypt, we announced the sale of our retail banking business. The completion of announced disposals and related actions remains subject to relevant regulatory and other approvals. We have commenced a strategic review of our retail business and domestic-focused small and mid-sized corporate banking operations in Türkiye. Our international wholesale client business in Türkiye is unaffected by this review. Ñ For further details on business disposals, see page 13 . Transaction banking continues to perform well as we leverage our network and capabilities to capture opportunities from changing trade and capital flows. In 9M26, fee and other income in WTB rose by 8% compared with 9M25, or by 6% on a constant currency basis, reflecting increases in Global Foreign Exchange, Securities Services, Global Trade Solutions (‘GTS‘) and Global Payment Solutions (‘GPS‘). Basis of presentation Constant currency performance Constant currency performance is computed by adjusting reported results for the effects of foreign currency translation differences, which reflect the movements of the US dollar against most major currencies during 2026. Excluding these differences allows us to assess balance sheet and income statement performance on a like-for-like basis and to better understand the underlying trends in the business. Foreign currency translation differences at 30 September 2026 are computed by retranslating into US dollars for non-US dollar branches, subsidiaries, joint ventures and associates: - the income statements for 9M25 at the average rate of exchange for 9M26; - the income statements for the quarterly periods at the average rate of exchange for 3Q26; - the closing prior period balance sheets at the prevailing rates of exchange on 30 September 2026. No adjustment has been made to the exchange rates used to translate foreign currency-denominated assets and liabilities into the functional currencies of any HSBC branches, subsidiaries, joint ventures or associates. The constant currency data of our operations in Türkiye has not been adjusted further for the impacts of hyperinflation. When reference is made to foreign currency translation differences in tables or commentaries, comparative data reported in the functional currencies of HSBC’s operations has been translated at the appropriate exchange rates applied in the current period on the basis described above. Notable items and material notable items We separately disclose ‘notable items’, which are components of our income statement that management would consider as outside the normal course of business and generally non-recurring in nature. Certain notable items are classified as ‘material notable items’, which are a subset of notable items. Categorisation as a material notable item is dependent on the nature of each item in conjunction with the financial impact on the Group‘s income statement, and such items are excluded from our target basis dividend payout ratio calculation and earnings per share measure. Material notable items in 9M26 or relevant comparative periods relate to the operating expenses associated with actions to exit or wind down non-strategic businesses. They also include a dilution loss and the recognition of an impairment of our investment in BoCom in 9M25, and a legal provision relating to developments in a claim in Luxembourg relating to the Bernard L. Madoff Investment Securities LLC fraud in 9M25. Ñ The tables on pages 25 to 29 and pages 32 to 36 detail the effects of notable items on each of our business segments and legal entities. Impact of strategic transactions In addition to the items categorised as material notable items, the impacts of strategic transactions include the distorting impact observed between the periods of the operating income statement results related to acquisitions, disposals and wind-downs that affect period-on-period comparisons. Once a transaction has completed or a wind-down has commenced, the impact will include the operating income statement results of each business, which are not classified as notable items, in any comparative period if there are no results in the current period as a result of a transaction, or a reduction in revenue or costs has arisen from the wind-down of a business. We consider the monthly impact of distorting income statement results when calculating the impact of strategic transactions. In the case of wind-downs, or transactions that complete in phased tranches, there may be timing differences between the recognition of operating cost impacts and operating revenue impacts. These would arise in the event that there is a timing lag between the impact of cost actions and the resultant impact on operating revenue. Ñ See page 30 for further details on the impact of strategic transactions. Impact of hyperinflationary accounting We continue to treat Türkiye as a hyperinflationary economy for accounting purposes. The impact of applying International Accounting Standard (‘IAS’) 29 ‘Financial Reporting in Hyperinflationary Economies’ and the hyperinflation provisions of IAS 21 ’The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates’ in the current period on our operations in Türkiye was a decrease in the Group’s profit before tax of $146m for 9M26 and a decrease of $40m for 3Q26 (9M25: $119m decrease; 2Q26: $44m decrease; 3Q25: $40m decrease). The consumer price index at 30 September 2026 for Türkiye was 137.23 (30 June 2026: 129.99; 30 September 2025: 105.78). These measures include those derived from our reported results that eliminate factors distorting period-on-period comparisons. Banking net interest income Banking net interest income (‘banking NII‘) adjusts our NII primarily for the impact of funding trading and fair value activities reported in interest expense. It represents the Group’s banking revenue that is directly impacted by changes in interest rates. We use this measure to determine the deployment of our surplus funding and to help optimise our structural hedging and risk management actions. Constant currency revenue and profit before tax excluding notable items and the impact of strategic transactions To aid the understanding of our results, we separately report ‘constant currency revenue excluding notable items‘ and ‘constant currency profit before tax excluding notable items‘, which exclude the impact of notable items and the impact of foreign exchange translation. We also separately disclose ‘constant currency revenue excluding notable items and the impact of strategic transactions‘ and ‘constant currency profit before tax excluding notable items and the impact of strategic transactions‘, which also exclude the impact of strategic transactions classified as material notable items. We consider these measures to provide useful information to investors as they remove items that distort period-on-period comparisons. The impact of strategic transactions also includes the distorting impact between the periods of the operating income statement results related to acquisitions and disposals and that affect period-on-period comparisons. These impacts are not included in our notable or material notable items. The impact of strategic transactions is computed by including the operating income statement results of each business in any period for which there are no results in the comparative period. We consider target basis operating expenses to provide useful information to investors by quantifying and excluding the notable items that management considered when setting and assessing cost-related targets. Risk Managing risk We maintain a proactive approach to managing our exposure to economic, financial and geopolitical risks, supported by continuous monitoring and review. Developments in these areas have historically affected, and may in the future adversely affect, HSBC’s customers, operations and overall risk profile, including financial risks. Geopolitical developments, particularly the conflict in the Middle East, remain a source of uncertainty and have resulted in periodic financial market volatility, higher and more volatile oil and gas prices, and supply chain disruption. This environment is contributing to higher inflation with associated upward pressure on interest rates, while increasing the risk of a slowdown in global growth. For some countries, particularly those in the Middle East, where oil and gas output has been disrupted and travel, logistics and other sectors are affected, the impact on growth is already significant. Supply chain disruptions have materialised in some key trade corridors and could have long-lasting implications for the direction of trade, and for energy, military and economic security. We keep developments under close review with a focus on potential impacts on our Gulf operations, as well as our customers and suppliers. We also continue to monitor the Russia-Ukraine war, including hybrid attacks across Europe attributed to Russia, and any indication of other potential military action or conflict elsewhere. Heightened strategic competition between the US and China, including cross-border investment restrictions, is also affecting the configuration of global supply chains, which may in turn affect the Group’s operations. Sanctions and restrictions on trade and investment are continually evolving in response to geopolitical events and may adversely affect the Group, our customers and the markets in which the Group operates. These factors may result in increased legal, regulatory, reputational and market risks, and a more complex operating environment. Trade and tariff policies remain a source of uncertainty for businesses and consumers. Changes to tariff rates, including sector-specific measures, could reduce capital investment and consumer spending, disrupt supply chains and dampen global trade growth. While the reconfiguration of trade and supply routes may create new opportunities for investment and growth, it could also have an adverse impact on the Group and our customers. We remain subject to interest rate risk, which can affect net interest income, the fair value of our assets and liabilities, and overall financial performance. Changes in inflation expectations, energy prices, fiscal policy and geopolitical developments may increase interest rate volatility by altering market expectations for the timing and pace of interest rate changes by major central banks. As inflationary pressures have increased, major central banks have adopted a more restrictive policy stance. At the beginning of 2026, markets expected both the US Federal Reserve and the Bank of England (‘BoE’) to reduce policy rates. However, in the year to date, the US Federal Reserve has raised its interest rate once, while the BoE has left its policy rate unchanged. Higher interest rates, or a prolonged period of restrictive policy, could reduce loan demand across key consumer and business segments, weaken credit quality and weigh on real estate and other asset prices. By contrast, lower interest rates, or faster than expected policy easing, could put downward pressure on net interest margins and adversely affect profitability. Interest rate uncertainty may contribute to broader market volatility, affecting funding costs, hedging effectiveness and the valuation of financial instruments. Exchange rate volatility may also affect our risk exposure through mark-to-market changes in trading positions and the translation effects of currency movements. Our risk profile may be influenced by fiscal policies, public deficits and levels of sovereign indebtedness. In many of our major markets, government debt has increased, reflecting higher social welfare commitments and expenditure on defence, energy security and climate transition. In the year to date, yields on long-term government debt have increased across major economies, amid concerns over fiscal outlooks and increased debt issuance. Higher sovereign borrowing costs could constrain fiscal capacity and adversely affect debt sustainability, particularly for highly indebted sovereigns. Elevated issuance and refinancing needs, together with changes in investor demand and market liquidity, could increase sovereign funding pressures. The growing role of non-bank financial institutions in holding and intermediating sovereign debt may amplify the transmission of market volatility, as leveraged positions can trigger forced sales when yields rise or funding conditions tighten, impairing liquidity across markets. Higher yields could also reduce the value of our sovereign bond holdings and increase private sector borrowing costs, as well as funding and refinancing risks for our customers and counterparties, with potentially negative implications for credit quality and market conditions. More broadly, the continued growth of private equity and debt markets, and their increasing interconnectedness with banks and other financial institutions, may amplify the transmission of credit, liquidity and valuation risks across the financial system. We continue to monitor investment and developments in the artificial intelligence (‘AI’) and technology sectors. While AI may improve productivity and support growth, there is a risk that the expected gains will fail to materialise or will take longer than expected. This could contribute to a disruptive correction to the valuations of AI and technology-related assets. AI adoption could also disrupt established business models and labour markets, potentially increasing unemployment in certain sectors and markets. These risks could affect HSBC’s risk profile and earnings by impairing the creditworthiness of borrowers, increasing the financial vulnerability of customers and decreasing the value of collateral and other claims. In Hong Kong, real estate market conditions continued to stabilise in the third quarter of 2026. The recovery nevertheless remains uneven, with improved leasing activity in prime office and retail locations but higher vacancies, valuation pressure and subdued investor appetite continuing in non-prime segments. Sentiment in the residential property market remains resilient. The recent increase in interest rates and inflationary pressure present a headwind to the broader recovery. Further increases could weaken sentiment and trigger a softening of demand. In the third quarter of 2026, management adjustments to ECL were applied to reflect sector or portfolio risks that are not fully captured by our models. We continue to monitor, and seek to manage, the potential implications of all the above developments on our customers and our business. Measurement uncertainty and sensitivity analysis of ECL estimates The recognition and measurement of ECL involves the use of significant judgement and estimation. We form multiple scenarios based on economic forecasts and distributional estimates and apply these to credit risk models to estimate future credit losses. The results are then probability-weighted to determine an unbiased ECL estimate. Management assessed the current economic environment, reviewed the latest economic forecasts and market indicators and discussed key risks and uncertainties before selecting the economic scenarios and their probability weightings. Management judgemental adjustments are used where modelled allowance for ECL does not fully reflect the identified risks and related uncertainty, or to capture significant late-breaking events. Methodology At 30 September 2026, four economic scenarios were used to calculate ECL. These scenarios were selected to capture the latest economic expectations and to articulate management‘s view of the range of risks and potential outcomes. Of those four standard scenarios, the Upside, Central and Downside are drawn from external consensus forecasts, market data and probability distributions that represent a range of potential economic outcomes. The fourth scenario, the Downside 2, represents management’s view of severe downside risks in the tail of the distribution. In the third quarter of 2026, downside scenarios were calibrated as global energy supply shocks, where an escalation in geopolitical tensions and a significant rise in oil prices cause inflation and interest rates to rise, leading to a downturn in economic activity subsequently. The scenarios are refreshed and updated with the latest economic forecasts and distributional estimates every quarter. Description of economic scenarios The consensus Central scenario reflects a baseline expectation that includes the effects of the rise in energy prices, inflation and higher interest rate expectations. The forecast assumes that growth across most of our major markets remains resilient to these factors and, while it slows, growth remains close to its trend rate. The main exception is the UAE, where the effect of the conflict in the Middle East on tourism, logistics and output is expected to have pushed the economy into recession this year. In the Central scenario, US GDP growth is expected to remain supported by ongoing AI-related investment and consumer spending, and the forecast, relative to the fourth quarter of 2025, has improved. In the UK, the outlook has deteriorated and GDP growth forecasts are lower, with the economy expected to slow as the effects of higher inflation and interest rates impact on consumer spending and the housing market. Relative to the fourth quarter of 2025, Hong Kong GDP forecasts have seen notable improvement as the territory has benefited from growth in trade-related demand for technology hardware and semiconductors, as well as demand for professional and financial services. Growth forecasts in the Chinese mainland have also improved as it benefits from growth in AI-related technology exports and gains global market share in other high value-added manufacturing sectors. The UAE remains the weakest economy and forecasts have been lowered due to the ongoing effects of the conflict in the Middle East on oil output and services activity. Risks to the Central scenario outlook are captured in the outer scenarios. The key sources of forecast uncertainty in the third quarter of 2026 include the impact on oil prices from the conflict in the Middle East and the risk to inflation and interest rates. Uncertain trade policies also remain a headwind and tariff escalation is also a key feature in the configuration of downside scenarios. The four global scenarios used for calculating ECL at 30 September 2026 were: – The consensus Central scenario: In this scenario, global growth is expected to slow moderately over the remainder of 2026, as higher energy costs raise consumer prices and central banks tighten policy. The global AI investment cycle is expected to continue and partly offset the effect of those headwinds on aggregate growth. O il prices are calibrated from market futures, which assume that prices decline gradually, that their inflationary effects are temporary and second order effects limited. Prices of Brent crude oil drop to $76 per barrel by the end of 2027. However, the US Federal Reserve, the BoE and European Central Bank are forecast to raise interest rates in the 12 months ahead to curb rising inflation risks. Consequently, activity and price growth in US, UK and European property markets, both residential and commercial, are expected to remain subdued. In Hong Kong, strong residential price growth is expected to moderate, while high vacancies and future supply are expected to constrain the commercial property recovery. Conditions in the property market in the Chinese mainland are expected to remain challenging. The US tariff rate, measured as an effective trade-weighted average, is assumed to be 9.7%, compared with 15% at the end of 2025. – The consensus Upside scenario: This scenario features faster growth in economic activity than the consensus Central scenario. In this scenario, trade and geopolitical tensions ease, causing tariffs and oil prices to fall more quickly than assumed in the Central scenario. Deregulation and technology-driven productivity gains also support demand. The improvement in confidence drives an acceleration in GDP growth and unemployment falls, while equity markets and house prices see accelerated gains. – The consensus Downside scenario: This scenario assumes the conflict in the Middle East continues for longer than expected and that disruption through the Strait of Hormuz intensifies, such that oil and gas flows remain disrupted for a prolonged period. Brent crude oil is projected to peak at $124 per barrel. At the same time, the scenario assumes that capital expenditure-driven growth, related to AI, stalls. As a consequence, inflation and policy interest rates are assumed to rise more quickly than in the Central scenario as higher energy costs pass through to other sectors of the economy. Long-term government bond yields also rise quickly. In the scenario, these combined pressures lead to a weakening of demand, a deceleration in global growth, higher unemployment and tighter credit conditions. Equity markets and house prices also come under pressure. The scenario is consistent with an assumed US tariff rate, measured as an effective trade-weighted average, of 17%. – The Downside 2 scenario: This scenario reflects management’s view of the tail-end of the economic distribution. It incorporates the simultaneous crystallisation of a number of risks that lead to a deep global recession, including escalation of geopolitical risks, a more prolonged disruption to energy supplies, and labour market disruption due to the advancement of AI. Oil prices increase sharply and peak at $132 per barrel, lifting inflation higher and causing central banks to raise interest rates. Higher inflation, rising short-term rates and concerns about sovereign debt issuance drive a sharp rise in government bond yields. The subsequent projected drop in demand leads to a rapid increase in unemployment, which then fails to recover back to pre-scenario levels. The scenario is consistent with an assumed US tariff rate, measured as an effective trade-weighted average, of 20%. Management judgemental adjustments In the context of IFRS 9, management judgemental adjustments are typically short-term increases or decreases to the modelled allowance for ECL at either a customer, segment or portfolio level where management believes allowances do not sufficiently reflect the credit risk/expected credit losses at the reporting date. These can relate to risks or uncertainties that are not reflected in the models and/or to any late-breaking events with significant uncertainty, subject to management review and challenge. The drivers of management judgemental adjustments continue to evolve with the economic environment and as new risks emerge. Further details can be found in the section ‘Management judgemental adjustments‘ on pages 120 and 121 of the Annual Report and Accounts 2025. Management judgemental adjustments are reviewed under the governance process for IFRS 9, as detailed in the section ‘Credit risk management’ on page 107 of the Annual Report and Accounts 2025. At 30 September 2026, total management judgemental adjustments for retail and wholesale loans increased the allowance for ECL by $0.1bn (31 December 2025: $0.2bn increase). Management judgemental adjustments for the wholesale portfolio remained stable at $0.1bn at 30 September 2026. The adjustments were driven primarily by uncertainties arising from the conflict in the Middle East, as well as market-specific uncertainties across several geographies. The scenario models capture some of the impact of the conflict in the Middle East, while the adjustments account for impacts arising above and beyond those already modelled for the conflict. There was no significant change in management judgemental adjustments compared with 31 December 2025. Management judgemental adjustments for the retail portfolio were not significant at 30 September 2026. Market-specific uncertainties in relation to the conflict in the Middle East were captured through management judgemental adjustments that included localised impacts for markets such as the UAE. Compared with 31 December 2025, there was a reduction in management judgemental adjustments in the UK and Mexico. Economic scenarios sensitivity analysis of ECL estimates Management considered the sensitivity of the ECL outcome against the economic forecasts as part of the ECL governance process by recalculating the ECL under each scenario described above for selected portfolios, applying a 100% weighting to each scenario in turn. The weighting is reflected in both the determination of a significant increase in credit risk and the measurement of the resulting ECL. The allowance for ECL calculated for the Upside and Downside scenarios should not be taken to represent the upper and lower limits of possible ECL outcomes. The impact of defaults that might occur in the future under different economic scenarios is captured by recalculating ECL for loans at the balance sheet date. There is a particularly high degree of estimation uncertainty in numbers representing more severe risk scenarios when assigned a 100% weighting. For wholesale credit risk exposures, the sensitivity analysis excludes allowance for ECL and financial instruments related to defaulted (stage 3) obligors. Loans to defaulted obligors are a small portion of the overall wholesale lending exposure, even if representing the majority of the allowance for ECL. The measurement of stage 3 ECL is relatively more sensitive to credit factors specific to the obligor than future economic scenarios, and therefore the effects of macroeconomic factors are not necessarily the key consideration when performing individual assessments of allowances for obligors in default. Due to the range and specificity of the credit factors to which the ECL is sensitive, it is not possible to provide a meaningful alternative sensitivity analysis for a consistent set of risks across all defaulted obligors. For retail credit risk exposures, the sensitivity analysis includes ECL allowance for loans and advances to customers related to defaulted obligors. This is because the retail ECL allowance for secured mortgage portfolios, including loans in all stages, is sensitive to macroeconomic variables. The analysis excludes portfolios utilising less complex modelling approaches.
