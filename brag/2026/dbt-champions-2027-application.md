## What contribution from the past year are you most proud of, and why?

The dbt Meetups organiser page. Running a meetup is mostly finding speakers. Most organisers ask the same people they already know. That keeps the line-ups narrow, and new voices rarely get asked.

The page lists the companies that use dbt in 25 cities and the people there who write about it. It ranks people who publish about dbt but have never spoken ahead of proven speakers. It also shows which topics a chapter has never covered. Any organiser can use it for free, without asking me.

I am proud of it because it helps chapters I will never attend. It also makes it easier for a first-time speaker to be asked.

## Tell us about a time you helped someone else learn, succeed, or feel included

At ReDI School, most of my students were migrants starting out in tech. Many had never written code. **[check]** Our teaching team of eight wrote a 13-week course that paired each lesson with a case-study project. I taught the classes across three semesters. About 80% of my class moved on to the next level. The school average was 50%.

What helped most was teaching students how to reason about a problem before writing code. I come from accounting, so I know what it is like to learn data work from the outside. I use the same approach at meetups. I talk to first-time attendees and ask what topics would have been useful to them.

## Why do you want to become a dbt Champion, and what do you hope to get out of it?

In my first year as a Champion, I mostly worked alone: my own meetups, my own talks, my own tool. In 2027 I want to work with the other Champions. Several of us run local chapters, and each chapter solves the same problems separately. We all need speakers, fresh topics and new people. I want to share what I built and learn how others solve these problems.

I also want to write more. My talks reach a room, but a written guide reaches people who could not attend.
