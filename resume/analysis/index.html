Question 01 of 06
Does this market care more about speed or correctness?
See the answer ↓
Finding 01

82% rigour-first. Zero pure velocity roles.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for repeated language around pace vs. correctness. "Rigour" = three or more signals of: TDD, CI/CD, production-grade, mocking, performance profiling, compliance, audit, meticulous, controlled. "Velocity" = move fast, bias for action, agile, iterate, pragmatic. "Mixed" = both present, neither dominant.

What the data shows: 82% rigour, 18% mixed, 0% pure velocity. The 18% mixed still lead with correctness — velocity is a secondary signal in all of them. Every mature data team in the corpus is rigour-only. This is the AE market's defining characteristic: it has collectively decided that speed without quality is not a trade-off worth making.

Resume implication: Default to risk-reduction framing. "Zero production incidents", "97% test coverage", "reduced data quality failures by X" outperform speed bullets in 82% of applications. Velocity language only helps in early-stage roles — filter by team maturity first.

Question 02 of 06
Who actually wrote this job description — and does it matter?
See the answer ↓
Finding 02

48% engineer-authored. Mixed authorship is the new normal.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for specificity of language. "Hiring manager" = named sub-tools (e.g. StarRocks, SparkSQL, PySpark), scale numbers (~1B rows daily, multi-terabyte), specific architectural patterns (incremental models, Data Mesh, medallion), or technical anti-patterns named explicitly. "Recruiter" = generic category language, soft-skill padding, no tool specificity. "Mixed" = technical core with recruiter boilerplate appended.

What the data shows: 48% hiring manager, 43% mixed, only 10% pure recruiter. The majority of AE JDs were written by, or heavily influenced by, someone who has done this job. Only 10% are pure boilerplate — meaning ATS keyword optimisation is rarely the whole game.

Resume implication: For hiring-manager JDs, mirror their exact technical vocabulary. If they wrote "dbt incremental models with snapshot strategy", your bullet should say "incremental models" — not "data transformation". For mixed JDs, lead with the technical specifics, close with the stakeholder language.

Question 03 of 06
Are you being hired to build something new, or fix something broken?
See the answer ↓
Finding 03

Build vs. fix split evenly. Most roles live in the middle.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for the dominant mission signal. "Greenfield" = build, establish, define, create from scratch, first analytics hire, design. "Fix / Scale" = improve, scale, maintain, modernise, optimise, inherited, continued implementation. "Mixed" = both present with no clear dominant signal.

What the data shows: 43% mixed, 29% greenfield, 29% fix/scale — almost perfectly three-way split. The majority of roles ask you to do both: extend existing infrastructure while establishing new capabilities. Crucially, strategic autonomy does not predict greenfield — only 5 of 21 strategic roles are pure greenfield. Most "own the analytics function" roles are building on top of something that already exists.

Resume implication: Versatility is the default expectation. Show both modes: "designed X from scratch" alongside "inherited Y, improved it to Z". Roles that want only one mode signal it clearly — and they're the minority.

Question 04 of 06
How mature is the data team you'd be joining?
See the answer ↓
Finding 04

61% mid-stage. Mature teams now 23% — more than expected.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for signals of the data team's stage. "Early" = first or only data hire implied, no team described, "build from zero", solo AE with no peers mentioned. "Mid" = small existing team, some structure present, "mentor junior engineers", "lead a team of 2". "Mature" = established engineering ladder implied by seniority level, multiple squads, "Staff" or "Principal" titles with cross-squad mandate.

What the data shows: 61% mid-stage, 23% mature, 16% early. The broader corpus reveals a significant cohort of established data organisations (Spotify, Netflix, Vinted, N26) that don't appear heavily in cold-apply pipelines. All 14 mature-team roles are rigour-only without exception.

Resume implication: Mature teams want evidence of operating within standards and raising them — governance contributions, cross-squad influence, architecture decisions that outlasted you. Early-stage teams want proof you can build without a net. Mid-stage teams — the majority — want both.

Question 05 of 06
Who would you actually be building for every day?
See the answer ↓
Finding 05

62% internal-facing. Commercial and finance split the rest.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for the primary audience named in the responsibilities section. Commercial = sales, GTM, customer success, revenue ops, partnerships. Product = product teams, growth, experimentation, A/B testing. Internal data = data platform, self-serve analytics, other analysts/engineers. Finance = FP&A, audit, P&L, controllership.

What the data shows: 62% internal data — the AE role is primarily a craft role, building infrastructure and models consumed by other data practitioners, not end-business stakeholders. Finance (13%) is 100% high-risk domain. Commercial (8%) and product (8%) are the minority. The "data-driven GTM" AE role is rarer than LinkedIn posts suggest.

Resume implication: For internal-data roles, lead with platform thinking: self-serve enablement, modelling standards, semantic layer, data contracts. For finance roles, precision and auditability outweigh everything. Commercial roles want business impact framing — pipeline influenced, revenue attributed, churn predicted.

Question 06 of 06
Will you set the direction, or follow it?
See the answer ↓
Finding 06

Strategic and execution split exactly evenly at 34% each.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for the verb choices in the responsibilities section. Strategic = "define", "own", "shape", "establish", "lead", "drive" — the role sets direction. Execution = "support", "assist", "deliver", "help", "contribute to" — direction comes from elsewhere. Mixed = owns a technical domain but serves a business team's requests.

What the data shows: 34% strategic, 34% execution, 31% mixed — the market does not sort neatly by seniority. "Senior Analytics Engineer" appears across all three categories. The difference is not the title but the framing: strategic roles say "you will define", execution roles say "you will support".

Resume implication: Read the verb tense before applying. For strategic roles, your bullets must show direction-setting: "designed the modelling framework", "established the data contract standard", "shifted the team from reactive to proactive". For execution roles, impact framing matters more: "reduced dashboard load time by 60%", "delivered 8 data products in Q1". Applying with the wrong register is a reliable rejection signal.

Finding 07

Berlin leads at 39%, but other Europe is a real second cluster.

Berlin leads at 39% but is not the whole story. Other Europe — Amsterdam, Paris, Zurich, Tallinn, Budapest, Lille — forms a genuine second cluster at 26%. The Nordics (Stockholm, Copenhagen, Oslo) add 13%, skewing toward scale-ups with more mature data teams. UK roles are almost absent from this corpus. Use the region filter to isolate any market — stakeholder orientation shifts noticeably by geography.

Finding 08

SQL and dbt are the floor. Everything else is negotiable.

SQL (98%) and dbt (79%) are the baseline — not knowing either disqualifies you from the vast majority of roles. Python follows at 59%, now effectively a third core requirement. After that, a genuine cliff: Snowflake (46%), Airflow (39%), Tableau (34%), Looker (31%). Power BI (28%) and Databricks (21%) are niche. Spark (11%), Kafka (5%), and Terraform (5%) appear rarely enough that listing them is a differentiator, not a gap if you lack them.
