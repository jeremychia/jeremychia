Question 01 of 09
Does this market care more about speed or correctness?
See the answer ↓
Finding 01

80% rigour-first. Only 2% pure velocity roles.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for repeated language around pace vs. correctness. "Rigour" = three or more signals of: TDD, CI/CD, production-grade, mocking, performance profiling, compliance, audit, meticulous, controlled. "Velocity" = move fast, bias for action, agile, iterate, pragmatic. "Mixed" = both present, neither dominant.

What the data shows: 80% rigour, 17% mixed, 2% pure velocity. The 17% mixed still lead with correctness — velocity is a secondary signal in all of them. Every mature data team in the corpus is rigour-only. This is the AE market's defining characteristic: it has collectively decided that speed without quality is not a trade-off worth making.

Resume implication: Default to risk-reduction framing. "Zero production incidents", "97% test coverage", "reduced data quality failures by X" outperform speed bullets in 80% of applications. Velocity language only helps in early-stage roles — filter by team maturity first.

Question 02 of 09
Who actually wrote this job description — and does it matter?
See the answer ↓
Finding 02

48% engineer-authored. Mixed authorship is the new normal.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for specificity of language. "Hiring manager" = named sub-tools (e.g. StarRocks, SparkSQL, PySpark), scale numbers (~1B rows daily, multi-terabyte), specific architectural patterns (incremental models, Data Mesh, medallion), or technical anti-patterns named explicitly. "Recruiter" = generic category language, soft-skill padding, no tool specificity. "Mixed" = technical core with recruiter boilerplate appended.

What the data shows: 48% hiring manager, 43% mixed, only 10% pure recruiter. The majority of AE JDs were written by, or heavily influenced by, someone who has done this job. Only 10% are pure boilerplate — meaning ATS keyword optimisation is rarely the whole game.

Resume implication: For hiring-manager JDs, mirror their exact technical vocabulary. If they wrote "dbt incremental models with snapshot strategy", your bullet should say "incremental models" — not "data transformation". For mixed JDs, lead with the technical specifics, close with the stakeholder language.

Question 03 of 09
Are you being hired to build something new, or fix something broken?
See the answer ↓
Finding 03

Build vs. fix split evenly. Most roles live in the middle.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for the dominant mission signal. "Greenfield" = build, establish, define, create from scratch, first analytics hire, design. "Fix / Scale" = improve, scale, maintain, modernise, optimise, inherited, continued implementation. "Mixed" = both present with no clear dominant signal.

What the data shows: 43% mixed, 29% greenfield, 29% fix/scale — almost perfectly three-way split. The majority of roles ask you to do both: extend existing infrastructure while establishing new capabilities. Crucially, strategic autonomy does not predict greenfield — only 5 of 21 strategic roles are pure greenfield. Most "own the analytics function" roles are building on top of something that already exists.

Resume implication: Versatility is the default expectation. Show both modes: "designed X from scratch" alongside "inherited Y, improved it to Z". Roles that want only one mode signal it clearly — and they're the minority.

Question 04 of 09
How mature is the data team you'd be joining?
See the answer ↓
Finding 04

58% mid-stage. Mature and early teams now tied at 20% each.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for signals of the data team's stage. "Early" = first or only data hire implied, no team described, "build from zero", solo AE with no peers mentioned. "Mid" = small existing team, some structure present, "mentor junior engineers", "lead a team of 2". "Mature" = established engineering ladder implied by seniority level, multiple squads, "Staff" or "Principal" titles with cross-squad mandate.

What the data shows: 58% mid-stage, 20% mature, 20% early. The broader corpus reveals a significant cohort of established data organisations (Spotify, Netflix, Vinted, N26) that don't appear heavily in cold-apply pipelines. All 17 mature-team roles are rigour-only without exception.

Resume implication: Mature teams want evidence of operating within standards and raising them — governance contributions, cross-squad influence, architecture decisions that outlasted you. Early-stage teams want proof you can build without a net. Mid-stage teams — the majority — want both.

Question 05 of 09
Who would you actually be building for every day?
See the answer ↓
Finding 05

60% internal-facing. Commercial and finance now tied at 12% each.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for the primary audience named in the responsibilities section. Commercial = sales, GTM, customer success, revenue ops, partnerships. Product = product teams, growth, experimentation, A/B testing. Internal data = data platform, self-serve analytics, other analysts/engineers. Finance = FP&A, audit, P&L, controllership.

What the data shows: 60% internal data — the AE role is primarily a craft role, building infrastructure and models consumed by other data practitioners, not end-business stakeholders. Finance (12%) is 100% high-risk domain. Commercial (12%) has grown; product (6%) remains the minority. The "data-driven GTM" AE role is rarer than LinkedIn posts suggest.

Resume implication: For internal-data roles, lead with platform thinking: self-serve enablement, modelling standards, semantic layer, data contracts. For finance roles, precision and auditability outweigh everything. Commercial roles want business impact framing — pipeline influenced, revenue attributed, churn predicted.

Question 06 of 09
Will you set the direction, or follow it?
See the answer ↓
Finding 06

Execution leads at 37%. Strategic and mixed nearly tied behind it.

How this was classified: Each JD was read for the verb choices in the responsibilities section. Strategic = "define", "own", "shape", "establish", "lead", "drive" — the role sets direction. Execution = "support", "assist", "deliver", "help", "contribute to" — direction comes from elsewhere. Mixed = owns a technical domain but serves a business team's requests.

What the data shows: 37% execution, 31% strategic, 30% mixed — the market does not sort neatly by seniority. "Senior Analytics Engineer" appears across all three categories. The difference is not the title but the framing: strategic roles say "you will define", execution roles say "you will support".

Resume implication: Read the verb tense before applying. For strategic roles, your bullets must show direction-setting: "designed the modelling framework", "established the data contract standard", "shifted the team from reactive to proactive". For execution roles, impact framing matters more: "reduced dashboard load time by 60%", "delivered 8 data products in Q1". Applying with the wrong register is a reliable rejection signal.

Question 07 of 09
Does the market actually want AI skills — or is it just saying so?
See the answer ↓
Finding 07

84% of JDs expect no AI skill from the candidate at all.

How this was classified: The question is what the candidate is expected to do with AI — not what the company builds. Three categories: none = no AI skill expected (includes companies building AI where the AE does standard modelling); ai_user = candidate uses AI coding tools (Copilot, Claude Code, Cursor) to accelerate their own work; ai_enabler = candidate builds data infrastructure that AI systems consume ("AI-ready foundations", "semantic model for AI", "text-to-SQL", "GenAI" in responsibilities).

What the data shows: 84% none, 15% ai_enabler, 1% ai_user (n=93). The "AI-first" discourse has not reached the job description layer at anywhere near the rate dbt Labs' own survey reports (72% of respondents claiming daily AI coding use). Vague phrases — "AI-first mindset", "excited about AI", "we use AI internally" — were explicitly coded as none, because they signal culture, not skill expectation. The small ai_enabler cohort (1 in 7 roles) consists of roles explicitly building data infrastructure for downstream AI consumption: semantic layers, ML feature pipelines, text-to-SQL readiness. Almost no roles (1 of 93) named AI coding tools as a skill requirement — meaning even where teams use Copilot or Claude Code daily, it has not yet become a hiring criterion.

Resume implication: Mentioning AI tool usage is not a differentiator in most of this market — it is simply not what they are screening for. For the ai_enabler cohort, semantic modelling and ML-adjacent data infrastructure work is the signal worth surfacing. For everyone else, quality, governance, and domain expertise outweigh AI enthusiasm in application screening.

Question 08 of 09
Who owns data quality — you, or a tool?
See the answer ↓
Finding 08

57% of JDs frame testing as something you own, not just a tool you know.

How this was classified: Three categories: responsibility = the candidate owns quality as a named accountability — action verbs like "own", "ensure", "define", "implement", "establish" paired with "quality", "testing", "data contracts", or "observability"; tool_listed = a testing tool appears in the required or preferred stack without ownership language; absent = no testing or quality signal anywhere in the JD.

What the data shows: 57% responsibility, 39% absent, 4% tool_listed (n=93). This is the closest the JD data comes to confirming dbt Labs' "trust gap" narrative directly — testing ownership has become a named hiring criterion in the majority of roles, not just a nice-to-have. It tracks closely with the 84% rigour-orientation finding (Q1): rigour-framed JDs are disproportionately the ones that also name testing as an owned responsibility, not just a stack item. The 39% absent group is concentrated in earlier-stage and velocity-leaning roles where the infrastructure to own doesn't yet exist.

Resume implication: For responsibility-framed roles, data quality ownership is a primary selection criterion — the majority case in this market. Lead with concrete accountability: "owned the data contract framework", "reduced production data incidents by X%", "defined testing standards for 40+ dbt models". For tool_listed roles, name the tool and the outcome — but don't over-index on quality; the JD is signalling familiarity, not mastery. For absent roles, don't bury the lede with quality bullets — lead with delivery and impact.

Question 09 of 09
What is this company actually afraid of?
See the answer ↓
Finding 09

60% fear operational failure. Only 15% are dominated by compliance fear.

How this was classified: Three levels of loss-aversion framing: none = JD framed in pure delivery and capability terms, no risk register; moderate = operational reliability is a secondary concern — fear is pipeline outages, SLOs, or data failures, not compliance; high = risk, compliance, or stakeholder trust framing dominates — fear is bad data reaching decision-makers or regulatory exposure.

What the data shows: 60% moderate, 25% none, 15% high (n=93). Pure delivery framing is the minority — three in four roles carry some fear signal. But the dominant fear is operational, not the compliance/trust framing dbt Labs' 2026 report leads with (71% citing fear of hallucinated outputs reaching stakeholders): "we can't have pipelines going down", "incidents need response", "production reliability matters". Compliance-dominated framing (high) is concentrated in finance-adjacent and regulated sectors — a narrower slice of the market than the survey's headline number implies. The none group is almost entirely early-stage or greenfield roles where the infrastructure does not yet exist to fail.

Resume implication: Read the fear register before framing your application. For high roles, open with risk-reduction proof: zero-incident records, audit trail implementation, compliance frameworks. For moderate roles — the majority case — reliability metrics ("99.X% pipeline uptime", "reduced on-call burden") resonate more than feature delivery. For none roles, pure delivery and impact framing — these companies aren't afraid yet.

Finding 10

Berlin leads at 36%, but other Europe is a real second cluster.

Berlin leads at 36% but is not the whole story. Other Europe — Amsterdam, Lisbon, Tallinn, Barcelona, Paris, Zurich — forms a genuine second cluster at 27%. UK roles are now 13% of the corpus, a notable presence. The Nordics (Stockholm, Copenhagen, Oslo) add 11%, skewing toward scale-ups with more mature data teams. Use the region filter to isolate any market — stakeholder orientation shifts noticeably by geography.

Finding 11

SQL and dbt are the floor. Everything else is negotiable.

SQL (93%) and dbt (70%) are the baseline — not knowing either disqualifies you from the vast majority of roles. Python follows at 55%, now effectively a third core requirement. After that, a genuine cliff: Power BI (31%), Snowflake (37%), Airflow (34%), Tableau (29%), Looker (27%). Databricks (20%) is niche. Spark (14%), Kafka (5%), and Terraform (5%) appear rarely enough that listing them is a differentiator, not a gap if you lack them.

Worth reading against the dbt Labs survey: dbt Labs' own "State of Analytics Engineering" report treats dbt as the field standard — but its survey sample is drawn from dbt's own community channels, where 76% of respondents already used dbt before answering. This dataset isn't filtered that way: it's job postings collected independent of any tool affiliation. 68% require dbt — real, but not universal. One in three analytics engineering roles in this market run on a stack without it: Databricks SQL, BigQuery-native pipelines, or Spark-first architectures. A survey distributed through dbt's own channels structurally cannot see that third of the market.
