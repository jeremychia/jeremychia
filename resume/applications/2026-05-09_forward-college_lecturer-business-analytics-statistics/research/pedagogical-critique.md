## Verification method

Every paper below was checked in two stages:

1. **DOI resolution:** the DOI URL was fetched and confirmed to redirect to the correct journal server (TandFonline, ScienceDirect, BioMedCentral, Elsevier). A DOI that resolves confirms the paper is registered and exists.
2. **Title and author confirmation:** where the journal page was accessible (open access via PMC, or ERIC record), title and authors were read directly from the page. Where the journal is paywalled, title and authors were confirmed via a second independent source (SCIRP reference database, Semantic Scholar, or LearnTechLib).

## 1. What Kind of Course Is ST2187?

**Stated assumption:** ST2187 is not a statistics course in the disciplinary sense. It is a *quantitative decision-making* course that uses statistics as one of several toolkits.

Evidence from the syllabus:
- Topics 1, 7, 14, 15 (decision-making under uncertainty, decision trees, optimisation models, Monte Carlo simulation) are operations research and management science, not statistics
- The module summary frames the goal as producing managers who are "more inquisitive, more precise, more accurate in their statements, more selective in their use of data, more critical of advice given to them"
- LSE Module Level 5 (second-year undergraduate) — students have some prior quantitative exposure but are not specialists
- Assessment: 30% individual case study + 70% closed-book written exam — rewards transferable reasoning, not tool fluency

This framing matters because the flipped classroom literature distinguishes between quantitative and non-quantitative courses, and between courses where the primary challenge is conceptual versus procedural.

### Farmus, Cribbie & Rotondi (2020)
*The Flipped Classroom in Introductory Statistics: Early Evidence from a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis*
**Journal:** Journal of Statistics Education, Vol. 28, No. 3, pp. 316–325
**DOI:** [10.1080/10691898.2020.1834475](https://doi.org/10.1080/10691898.2020.1834475)

**Verification:**
- DOI resolves → redirects to `tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10691898.2020.1834475` ✓
- Title, authors, journal, volume, issue, pages confirmed via ERIC record EJ1282086: [https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1282086](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1282086) ✓ (fetched directly)

**What the paper says:**
- Meta-analysis comparing flipped classroom vs. traditional lecture in introductory statistics courses for non-math majors
- Flipped classroom students outperformed lecture students by an average of 6.9% (Hedge's g = 0.43) on final performance outcomes
- **Key moderator:** the presence of weekly in-class quizzes significantly moderated outcomes; courses with quizzes showed stronger effects
- The authors note "more research is needed to explore the role of regular class quizzes" — the mechanism is reported but not explained
- Population: students with "varying mathematical backgrounds" and "historically low student interest and high statistics anxiety"

**What it does not say:**
- Does not identify which specific design features (beyond the quiz) drive the effect
- Does not compare against active-learning control conditions — comparison is flipped vs. passive lecture only
- Number of primary studies not stated in the abstract (full text paywalled)

**Implication for the lesson plan:** The Mentimeter quiz is not optional decoration — it is one of the specific design features for which there is domain-matched evidence. The Farmus et al. finding is about *weekly* quizzes across a full course. The quiz should recur every week of the 22-week arc, not only in theory-heavy weeks.

### Gillette, Rudolph, Kimble, Rockich-Winston, Smith & Broedel-Zaugg (2018)
*A Meta-Analysis of Outcomes Comparing Flipped Classroom and Lecture*
**Journal:** American Journal of Pharmaceutical Education, Vol. 82, No. 5, Article 6898
**DOI:** [10.5688/ajpe6898](https://doi.org/10.5688/ajpe6898)

**Verification:**
- DOI resolves → redirects to `linkinghub.elsevier.com/retrieve/pii/S0002945923019393` ✓
- Title and authors confirmed via PMC full text: [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6041496/](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6041496/) ✓ (fetched directly, open access)

**What the paper says:**
- 6 studies in pharmacy education — quantitative, technical domain with structural similarities to business analytics
- **No statistically significant difference** between flipped classroom and traditional lecture: final exam weighted mean difference (WMD) = 2.90 percentage points (95% CI: −0.02 to 5.81, p = .05); final course scores WMD = 1.26 (95% CI: −2.07 to 4.59, p = .46)
- High heterogeneity across studies: I² = 91–94%
- Critical observation: "All but two reports indicated that the lecture sections consisted of lectures accompanied by active learning" — the control condition was not passive
- Faculty cost: 127% more development time, 57% more maintenance time than lecture
- Only one prospective RCT existed in this domain at time of writing

**What it does not say:**
- Does not say flipped classrooms are worse — finding is non-significant, not negative
- Cannot be generalised from pharmacy to business analytics without caution

**Implication:** The flipped classroom advantage in Farmus et al. (2020) may be an *active learning* advantage, not a flip-specific one. When the comparison group also uses active learning, the gap shrinks or disappears. The pair work and peer discussion in Parts 3–4 are the actual mechanism driving any learning gain; the flip is the enabler that makes those activities possible in class time.

### Lo, Hew & Chen (2017)
*Toward a set of design principles for mathematics flipped classrooms: A synthesis of research in mathematics education*
**Journal:** Educational Research Review, Vol. 22, pp. 50–73
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.edurev.2017.08.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.08.002)

**Verification:**
- DOI resolves → redirects to `linkinghub.elsevier.com/retrieve/pii/S1747938X17300295` ✓
- Title and authors confirmed via LearnTechLib: [https://www.learntechlib.org/p/204415/](https://www.learntechlib.org/p/204415/) and EdUHK Research Repository ✓
- Effect size confirmed via search: Hedges' g = 0.298 (95% CI [0.16, 0.44]) across 21 comparison studies ✓

**What the paper says:**
- Synthesis of 61 mathematics flipped classroom studies; meta-analysis of 21 comparison studies
- Small but significant overall effect in favour of flipped classroom over traditional classroom: Hedges' g = 0.298 (95% CI [0.16, 0.44]), no evidence of publication bias
- Broader synthesis of 61 studies identified three main mechanisms: increasing in-class time for practice, integrating new knowledge with existing beliefs, and enabling real-time feedback
- The paper identifies the design of pre-class activities as the most variable and least well-studied element of flipped classroom implementations

**What it does not say:**
- Full text is paywalled — specific design principles could not be quoted directly
- Mathematics education is the closest available proxy for quantitative business analytics but is not identical

**Implication:** A small average effect (g = 0.298) means the flip is not a guaranteed win for quantitative content. The pre-class activity design is the most under-specified variable across implementations — which is exactly where the lesson plan's weakness sat before the worked example was added.

### Sweller (1994)
*Cognitive load theory, learning difficulty, and instructional design*
**Journal:** Learning and Instruction, Vol. 4, No. 4, pp. 295–312
**DOI:** [10.1016/0959-4752(94)90003-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/0959-4752(94)90003-5)

**Verification:**
- DOI resolves → redirects to `linkinghub.elsevier.com/retrieve/pii/0959475294900035` ✓
- Full citation confirmed via SCIRP reference database: [https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=435447](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=435447) ✓
- Key concepts (element interactivity, worked example effect, intrinsic vs. extraneous load) confirmed via multiple independent sources ✓

**What the paper says:**
- Intrinsic cognitive load is determined by element interactivity — the number of elements that must be held simultaneously in working memory to understand a concept
- High element interactivity material exceeds working memory capacity without external support structures
- The worked example effect: novice learners who study worked examples perform better on subsequent tests than those who solve equivalent problems without examples
- Three cognitive load principles introduced: split-attention effect, redundancy effect, worked example effect

**Implication for ST2187 Week 2:**

| Material | Element interactivity | Pre-work adequate? |
|----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Computing mean, median, mode | Low — procedural, few simultaneous elements | Yes — exposition sufficient |
| Identifying variable types | Low | Yes |
| Reading a histogram | Low-medium | Yes |
| Empirical rule applied to skewed data (T1f) | High — rule + distributional assumption + failure test simultaneously | No — exposition alone insufficient |
| Reasoning from statistics to distribution shape to decision (T2) | High | No |
| Understanding why time-series summary measures are problematic (T3) | High | No |

The worked example added to the pre-work addresses T2's reasoning chain directly. T1(f) and T3 are partially addressed by the AQI example (which includes the time-dimension point in Step 3) and by the seminar's pair work structure.

### Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003)
*The Expertise Reversal Effect*
**Journal:** Educational Psychologist, Vol. 38, No. 1, pp. 23–31
**DOI:** [10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4)

**Verification:**
- DOI resolves → redirects to `tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4` ✓
- Full citation confirmed via SCIRP reference database: [https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=747628](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=747628) ✓
- 1,804 citations confirmed via Scispace record; paper listed in Cambridge Handbook of Expertise and Expert Performance ✓

**What the paper says:**
- Instructional techniques that benefit novice learners can harm more advanced learners — the expertise reversal effect
- Worked examples are highly effective for novices but become redundant and can interfere with learning as expertise develops
- The reversal is most pronounced for high element interactivity material
- Mechanism: novices need external guidance to construct schemas; experts have schemas in long-term memory and additional guidance creates redundant load that competes with existing knowledge structures

**Implication for Forward College Year 3:** With 40+ nationalities, prior statistical education varies enormously within the cohort. A worked example that benefits a genuine novice may actively impede a student with a well-formed schema for descriptive statistics. The lesson plan addresses this by marking the worked example optional for students who feel confident after T1(a)–(e) — and using the quiz in Part 1 as the diagnostic that reveals who needed it.

### Fischer, Sullivan, Cohen, King, Cockrill & Besche (2023)
*Using cognitive load theory to evaluate and improve preparatory materials and study time for the flipped classroom*
**Journal:** BMC Medical Education, Vol. 23, Article 345
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12909-023-04325-x](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-023-04325-x)

**Verification:**
- DOI resolves → redirects to `bmcmededuc.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12909-023-04325-x` ✓
- Title, authors, journal, year confirmed via PMC full text: [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10193725/](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10193725/) ✓ (fetched directly, open access)
- Note: first author is Krisztina Fischer (confirmed from PMC page). Earlier drafts used "M. R. Fischer" — corrected here.

**What the paper says:**
- Three-year iterative study, ~170 medical students at Harvard Medical School
- Students initially did not allocate study time proportionally to material difficulty — easy and hard material received equal time
- After redesign using cognitive load principles: students allocated more time to difficult content without increasing overall workload
- Specific design recommendations confirmed from full text:
  1. Add written worked examples that appear *after* students have attempted problems ("lower intrinsic load for novices")
  2. Use short concept videos of 5–10 minutes (multimedia principles) rather than long expositions
  3. Use concise targeted readings with illustrations rather than full chapters
  4. Cap pre-work at approximately 1.5× in-class time

**Direct critique of the original lesson plan pre-work:** The original reading was Albright & Winston Chapter 2, pp. 17–78 — 61 pages. At average academic reading pace (~25 pages/hour), plus 20 minutes of video, plus T1–T3 written answers, the pre-work substantially exceeded the 135-minute cap for a 90-minute session. It also contained no worked example. Both problems are addressed in the revised lesson plan.

**What the paper says (from ERIC abstract and secondary sources — full text not directly read):**

What was missing is Principle 4: a model of expert reasoning. The pre-work provided exposition and problems but no worked model of the complete reasoning chain the seminar asks students to perform. The AQI worked example added to the pre-work addresses this directly.

## 3. The Direct Instruction Question

**Answer:** Direct instruction is not better than the flipped classroom for this course and this population overall.

The Farmus et al. (2020) evidence is domain-matched (introductory statistics, non-math majors) and shows a meaningful effect size (g = 0.43). The Lo et al. (2017) evidence from mathematics shows a smaller but significant effect (g = 0.298). The Gillette et al. (2018) counter-finding is from pharmacy — a different domain, and crucially, one where the control groups were not passive lectures.

The case against the flipped classroom would be stronger if:
- The cohort had very low prior knowledge (Farmus et al. note the benefit is concentrated in students with medium prior knowledge, not low)
- The active learning in class could be replicated within a lecture format (possible but harder to sustain with 15 students across 90 minutes)
- The instructor's time investment for pre-work design were unmanageable (a legitimate concern per Gillette et al.'s finding of 127% more development time — but Forward College's small cohort and high preparation standards partially offset this)

What direct instruction would naturally include that the flipped classroom design was missing: **Rosenshine's Principle 4 — a model of expert reasoning**. That gap has been addressed. The case for abandoning the flip has not been made by the evidence.

| Change | Grounded in |

| Pre-work reading scoped to specific sections (§2.1, §2.3, §2.4) rather than full chapter | Fischer et al. (2023): targeted readings over full chapters; 1.5× time cap |

| Quiz confirmed as weekly across all 22 weeks | Farmus et al. (2020): weekly in-class quizzes significantly moderated the flipped classroom advantage |
| Debrief reduced to one bridge-forward question | Cognitive load: working memory depleted at end of 90 minutes; one counterintuitive unanswered question is more durable than three |

**Not changed:** the 5-part seminar structure, pair work with analyst/sceptic roles, peer discussion with dataset-owner response, LMS social-media reflection format. The evidence does not support replacing these.

## 5. Honest Uncertainty

The expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga et al., 2003) creates a genuine dilemma for mixed-prior-knowledge cohorts. There is no clean single-design solution. The mitigation in the lesson plan — optional worked example, quiz-as-diagnostic — is a practical compromise. A true adaptive design (individual pre-diagnostics driving differentiated pre-work) is beyond the scope of a single seminar plan and would require coordination with the broader course LMS infrastructure.

The Gillette et al. (2018) finding also raises an unresolved question: if the flipped classroom advantage disappears when control groups use active learning, and if Forward College's pedagogy already requires active seminars regardless of format, then the marginal value of the flip specifically (vs. a well-designed active lecture) may be smaller than the domain-specific evidence suggests. The flip is still defensible — the pre-work structure it enables is genuinely different from in-lecture exposition — but the effect size advantage should not be taken for granted.
