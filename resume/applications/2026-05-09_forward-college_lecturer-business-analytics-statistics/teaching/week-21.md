# Flipped Classroom Lesson Plan
## ST2187 Business Analytics — Week 21: Full Analysis 3 — Challenge and Revise
**Format:** 90-minute seminar, 12–15 students in groups of 3–4

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:
- Formulate a precise analytical challenge to another group's methodology or interpretation
- Defend or revise their own analysis in response to a challenge, with explicit reasoning about what changed and why
- Distinguish between a challenge that requires a revision and one that reveals a limitation the analysis should acknowledge but cannot fix
- Produce a final revised analysis narrative that a non-specialist stakeholder could evaluate

This is Week 3 of the four-week full analysis cycle. The session is the formal challenge round: every presenting group faces a structured critique from a peer group, then defends or revises. By the end of the session, every group should know what their final Week 22 presentation will say — including what it cannot claim.

---

## Before Class (Student Pre-Work)

**Group preparation:**

Between Week 20 and Week 21, each group prepares two things:

**1. Revised analysis:** incorporating any changes made during Week 20's revision time and any additional revisions made since. Submit an updated group brief (same 1-page format, updated content) before class.

**2. Challenge brief for the assigned target group:** each group has been assigned a target group to challenge (rotating assignment from Week 20 results — the instructor assigns based on where the most productive analytical tension lies). The challenge brief contains:
- Three specific questions about the target group's methodology or interpretation
- For each question: what would a satisfactory answer look like? What would an unsatisfactory answer look like?
- One alternative analytical approach the target group could have used — and why it might give a different answer

The challenge should be constructive and precise, not vague. "Your p-value might be wrong" is not a challenge. "Your t-test assumes normally distributed residuals; did you check that assumption, and if not, how does that affect your confidence interval?" is a challenge.

---

## In-Class Session (90 minutes)

### Part 1 — Opening Framing (5 minutes)

The instructor states the session's standard:

*"The analyst's job is not to be right. It is to know what would make them wrong — and to say so clearly. Today's question for every group is: what did you find, what can you claim from it, and what can't you claim?"*

The format: each group presents for 3 minutes, the challenging group has 5 minutes to challenge, the presenting group has 5 minutes to respond, the class has 2 minutes to add. Repeat for all groups. Total: approximately 15 minutes per group (rounds adjusted to time).

---

### Part 2 — Formal Challenge Round (55 minutes)

Each group cycle (approximately 15 minutes per group, 3–4 groups = 45–60 minutes):

**[3 minutes] Presenting group:** summary of analysis. Not the description — the model, the result, and the interpretation. One slide or projected notebook is sufficient. The point of this summary is to re-establish what the group claims, not to re-present everything.

**[5 minutes] Challenging group:** three prepared challenges, read directly from their brief. They are not asking questions — they are making arguments. Format: "We believe [X] is a problem with your analysis because [Y]. A satisfactory response would be [Z]."

**[5 minutes] Presenting group response:** they must respond to each challenge:
- Accept: "Yes, this is a limitation. Here is how we've addressed it / why we couldn't address it."
- Dispute: "We disagree because [reason]. Here is the evidence from our analysis."
- Qualify: "This is a valid concern for [condition], but not for our specific question because [reason]."

**[2 minutes] Class:** one additional challenge or one observation. The class should have been thinking during the exchange, not just watching.

**Instructor role during the challenge round:** note-taker and timekeeper. Do not intervene unless a factual error about the course content is made. The groups own this exchange.

---

### Part 3 — Revision and Final Narrative (20 minutes)

Each group has 20 minutes to:
1. Identify which challenges they are incorporating as revisions
2. Identify which challenges they are noting as limitations (can't fix, but must acknowledge)
3. Draft the final narrative in 3–4 sentences: "We asked ___. We found ___. This is reliable/limited because ___. If we had more data/time, we would ___."

The 3–4 sentence narrative is the abstract of their Week 22 presentation. It should be specific enough that a stranger could evaluate it without context.

**Group log entry for this week:**

"The most challenging critique we received was ___. We responded by [accepting/disputing/qualifying] because ___. Our final claim is now ___. The most important limitation we are acknowledging is ___."

---

### Part 4 — Debrief (10 minutes)

**Close the loop:**

*"What changed between Week 19 and right now? Not the analysis — the claim. What did you start by claiming, and what can you actually claim?"*

This is the session's most important question. For most groups, the claim will be narrower and more specific than it was in Week 19. That's progress, not failure — it means the analysis has confronted its own limits.

**Preview of Week 22:**

> *"Next week is the formal presentation. You have 10 minutes plus 5 minutes of Q&A. The audience includes [an external evaluator / the cohort / a panel — specify based on course administration]. Your task is to present your final claim, your evidence, and your stated limitations. The question you'll be asked — somewhere in the Q&A — is 'what would you do differently with more time, more data, or a different model?' Be ready with a specific answer, not a vague one."*

---

## Design Rationale

| Design choice | Pedagogical grounding |
|---|---|
| Formal challenge round with written challenge brief | Johnson & Johnson (1988): structured controversy produces deeper understanding than unstructured debate; the written brief forces challengers to commit to specific claims before the session, preventing vague or personal challenges |
| Presenting group must respond using accept/dispute/qualify | This framework forces analytical reasoning rather than defensive reaction; students who can say "yes, this is a limitation, and here's why we can't fix it" demonstrate more understanding than students who defend every choice uncritically |
| 20-minute revision and final narrative | Constructivist learning (Piaget, 1952): knowledge constructed through the challenge-response-revision cycle is more durable than knowledge received passively; the narrative draft makes the revision concrete and presentable |
| Instructor as note-taker during challenge round, not intervenor | Vygotsky (1978): ZPD — the peer challenge produces learning that the instructor challenge in Week 20 primed; the instructor's role shifts from challenger to observer; intervening would collapse the ZPD |
| "Claim narrowed, not widened" as the Week 21 marker of progress | Bjork (1994): the most important learning in analysis is knowing what you cannot claim; Week 21's progress metric is specificity and honesty of the final claim, not volume of analysis |

## Pedagogical Design Challenges

### 1. The challenge round may devolve into politeness rather than precision.

Year 3 students who know each other well may soften their challenges to avoid discomfort. A challenge of "this is very interesting but maybe you could consider..." is not useful.

**Resolution:** the written challenge brief, submitted before class, commits the challenging group to specific questions. The instructor reviews all briefs before class and during Part 1 says: "I've read all the challenge briefs. The strongest ones named specific assumptions and gave specific alternative interpretations. Those are the standard." Naming the standard before the round begins raises the floor.

---

### 2. Groups who are challenged correctly may feel defensive.

If a challenge is accurate and significant — "your sample isn't random, so your t-test doesn't generalise" — the presenting group may feel their work has been invalidated rather than improved.

**Resolution:** the accept/dispute/qualify framework is the tool. Accepting a challenge is not failure — it is intellectual honesty. The instructor should model this in Week 20's challenge round: "The group that accepts a valid challenge and states it clearly in their final narrative is doing better analysis than the group that disputes everything." Year 3 students have had two years of feedback culture at Forward College — they should be equipped for this.

---

### 3. The 15-minute-per-group cycle may not fit evenly into 55 minutes.

With 3 groups, the round takes 45 minutes; with 4 groups, it takes 60 minutes.

**Resolution:** groups of 3–4 members; if there are 4 groups, shorten the class debrief (Part 4) to 5 minutes or compress Part 3 to 15 minutes. The revision and final narrative can extend into the group's prep time between Week 21 and Week 22 if the in-session time runs short. The challenge round is the non-negotiable element — do not compress it to fit a debrief.

---

### 4. The 3–4 sentence final narrative is harder to write than students expect.

"We studied the relationship between housing prices and income" is not a narrative — it's a topic description. "We found that a 1% increase in median income is associated with a 1.8% increase in housing prices in Berlin neighbourhoods, controlling for distance to the city centre (β = 1.8, p < 0.01, R² = 0.73), though this result should be treated with caution as the sample is cross-sectional and cannot support causal claims" is a narrative.

**Resolution:** provide the sentence template on the board: "We asked [specific question]. We found [specific result with measure of uncertainty]. This finding [is reliable / is limited] because [one key assumption or data quality issue]. If we could extend this analysis, we would [specific next step]."

# Supplement (2026-07-06): Textbook Cross-Reference, Extended Materials, Alternative Activities, Critique

## 1. Textbook Cross-Reference — Albright & Winston, 6th ed.

**Appendix A-2c/d/e (Be Clear / Be Concise / Be Precise) is the editing standard for Part 3's 3–4 sentence narrative** — the Design Challenge 4 template ("We asked… We found… This is limited because…") is exactly what A-2e's precision guidance produces. Assign these three pages as the pre-work's only reading; groups then edit their narrative against a published standard instead of instructor taste. The A-3 example reports remain the Week 22 reference models (per the Week 19 supplement).

## 2. Extended Materials (with model answers)

**E1 — Challenge quality ladder (the missing rubric for the challenge brief).** Show three versions of the same challenge; the brief must reach level 3:

- *Level 1 (vague — inadmissible):* "Your p-value might be wrong."
- *Level 2 (specific):* "Your t-test assumes independent observations, but your rents cluster by neighbourhood."
- *Level 3 (specific + standard of satisfaction):* "Your t-test assumes independence, but rents cluster by neighbourhood, which understates your standard error. A satisfactory response shows the conclusion survives when tested at the neighbourhood level (or with clustered SEs); an unsatisfactory response re-asserts the original p-value."

The level-3 shape ("problem + mechanism + what would satisfy us") is already mandated in the pre-work; the ladder makes the gap between levels visible and gives the instructor a one-word vocabulary for feedback on the briefs ("this one's a level 2 — add the satisfaction standard").

**E2 — Accept / dispute / qualify, worked micro-cases:**

1. Challenge: "Your 3 comparable sales can't support a Normal(320k, 40k) price distribution." Model **accept**: "Correct — we've re-run with a wider SD and a lognormal alternative; P(loss) rises from 8% to 14–19%, and our narrative now reports the range." (Acceptance = revision + changed claim, not apology.)
2. Challenge: "Your significant result is just multiple testing — you ran six tests." Model **dispute**: "We pre-specified this single hypothesis in our Week 19 contract — the other five tests were exploratory and labelled as such in the brief; the Week 13 pre-registration logic applies." (Dispute = citing evidence, here the documented question contract.)
3. Challenge: "Observational data — you can't claim the bike lane reduced accidents." Model **qualify**: "Agreed for causality — our claim is the association, stated with the time-confound limitation; a causal answer would need the before/after comparison across matched streets we list as the next step." (Qualify = conceding scope while defending the narrower claim.)

**E3 — Revision vs limitation decision rule (Part 3, step 1–2 currently gives no criterion).** A challenge becomes a **revision** if it can be executed with the data in hand before Week 22 (add a control, correct a test, report an effect size, run the robustness check). It becomes a **limitation** if fixing it requires data, time, or design you don't have (non-random sample, unmeasured confound, single year of data) — in which case it must appear *verbatim* in the final narrative's limitation clause. The failure mode to name: treating an executable fix as a "limitation" because the group is tired — the instructor's note-taking during Part 2 should flag any challenge that was accepted-as-limitation but is actually a 20-minute fix.

## 3. Alternative In-Class Activities (additional options)

**A. Steelman gate (add to Part 2, costs ~30 s per cycle).** Before responding, the presenting group must restate the challenge in their own words until the challengers confirm "that's our point." Defensive responses usually attack a misheard version of the challenge; the restatement rule makes that impossible, and it is the single cheapest de-escalation device for Design Challenge 2's defensiveness risk.

**B. Silent minute (add between challenge and response).** The presenting group writes for 60 seconds before speaking — deciding accept/dispute/qualify per challenge on paper. First spoken reactions are the most defensive ones; a written beat produces the analytical response the framework wants.

**C. Best-challenge ballot (5 min, in the debrief — delivers Week 20's promise).** Week 20's preview committed: "the group whose challenge is most incisive… will be named." Nothing in this session delivers that. Run a one-minute class vote (not for the harshest — for the challenge that most changed the target group's final narrative, which the log entries record). Naming the winner anchors the incentive for challenge quality that Week 20 created.

**D. Spectator role cards (Part 2).** The two groups not in the current cycle each get a job: one tracks the response framework (did each response actually accept/dispute/qualify, or evade?), the other drafts the "class challenge" for the 2-minute slot. Fifteen-minute cycles × 4 means up to 45 minutes of spectating per student otherwise; roles convert watching into scoring.

**E. Narrative red-pen swap (Part 3, final 5 min).** Groups exchange draft narratives and underline every word a stranger couldn't verify from the analysis ("clearly," "strong," "significant" without a number). Returned with underlines only — no discussion needed. Enforces A-2e precision at zero instructor cost.

## 4. Critique of the Lesson Plan

**What works (keep):** the written challenge brief with satisfaction standards (the block's best pre-work artefact); accept/dispute/qualify as a response grammar; the instructor's deliberate silence during Part 2 (correctly reasoned in the rationale); "the claim narrowed — that's progress" as the week's metric; Design Challenge 4's narrative template.

**Problems, reasons, and fixes:**

1. **An unresolved placeholder is printed in the student-facing text.** The Week 22 preview reads "[an external evaluator / the cohort / a panel — specify based on course administration]" — the third file to defer this decision (Weeks 19 and 20 assert the evaluator exists). *Fix:* decide once, then update Weeks 19, 20, 21, and 22 together. Recommendation: invite one external guest (a Berlin practitioner — squarely within Forward's Year 3 professional-exposure model and the Beta House precedent in the student-voice research); if impossible, the fallback is the instructor playing a briefed external role, which should then be *said*, not left bracketed.
2. **Week 20's "most incisive challenge will be named" promise has no mechanism here.** Activity C is the two-minute fix; without it, a stated incentive silently evaporates — the exact pattern (promise in week N, no delivery in week N+1) this review has flagged between Weeks 3→7 and 8→9.
3. **The plan never says whether presenting groups see the challenge briefs in advance.** Briefs are "submitted before class," but to whom is undefined — and the two designs teach different things (surprise tests thinking-on-feet; advance disclosure tests depth of revision). *Fix:* state it. Recommended: briefs go to the instructor only, but the challenge *archetypes* are public (per the Week 20 supplement) — groups can prepare for the type, not the instance, which matches how real review works.
4. **The revised Week 20 brief is submitted but nothing consumes it.** The instructor is note-taker only in Part 2, so the revised brief has no feedback channel this week. *Fix:* written margin comments returned within 48 hours as Week 22 preparation input — otherwise the submission is ritual.
5. **No criterion for the revision-vs-limitation split** that Part 3 hinges on (E3 supplies one). Without it, tired groups will misfile fixable problems as limitations, and Week 22's "honest limitation" standard gets quietly diluted.
6. **Spectator load.** Up to 45 minutes of watching per student across the four cycles (see Activity D). The 2-minute class slot presumes attention the format doesn't otherwise sustain.
