# Flipped Classroom Lesson Plan
## ST2187 Business Analytics — Week 22: Full Analysis 4 — Final Presentation
**Format:** 90-minute seminar + Q&A, 12–15 students in groups of 3–4

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:
- Deliver a 10-minute analytical presentation to a non-specialist audience that includes a question, a method, a result, and a stated limitation
- Respond to Q&A challenges with analytical precision — defending conclusions where justified, acknowledging limitations where not
- Evaluate their own analysis against the course standard: was the question specific? Was the method appropriate? Was the conclusion honest about uncertainty?
- Articulate what they would do differently with more data, more time, or a different model

This is the capstone session of ST2187. No new content is introduced. The session is the formal presentation of the four-week full analysis cycle. Students who have followed the arc from Week 19 to Week 22 will present analyses that have been through three rounds of refinement (description, modelling, peer challenge). The standard is not perfection — it is analytical honesty.

---

## Before Class (Student Pre-Work)

**Group preparation:**

Each group prepares a 10-minute presentation with slides or a projected notebook. The presentation must cover all four elements from the 3–4 sentence narrative drafted in Week 21:

1. **The question** (1 minute): specific, decision-relevant, one sentence
2. **The data and description** (2 minutes): where the data came from, what it shows, what assumptions are embedded in using it
3. **The method and result** (4 minutes): what method was used, why, what the result was (with appropriate uncertainty: CI, p-value, R², RMSE, or equivalent), and interpretation in plain language
4. **Limitations and next steps** (2 minutes): what the analysis cannot claim, one specific next step that would improve it, one question it raises that the current data cannot answer
5. **Q&A** (5 minutes): prepared by the group; they anticipate the three most likely questions and have thought-through answers

**Submit the final presentation before class.** The instructor reviews for completeness and flags any group that has not incorporated a limitation statement — that group receives a pre-class note: "Your presentation is missing the limitation. That is a required element. Add it."

---

## In-Class Session (90 minutes)

### Session Structure

| Activity | Time |
|---|---|
| Opening (instructor) | 5 min |
| Group presentations (3–4 groups × 15 min including Q&A) | 45–60 min |
| Reflective close | 10–15 min |
| Individual written reflection | 10 min |

The session timing depends on group count. Full schedules (including the opening and individual reflection):
- 3 groups: 5 opening + 45 presentations + 15 reflective close + 10 individual reflection = 75 min — use the spare 15 minutes to extend each Q&A to 7–8 minutes (5 + 54 + 15 + 10 = 84).
- 4 groups: 5 opening + 60 presentations + 10 reflective close + 10 individual reflection = 85 min. Shorten each description section to 90 seconds to protect the buffer.

---

### Opening (5 minutes)

The instructor addresses the room:

*"Four weeks ago, you picked a dataset and three questions. Today you're presenting what you found — and more importantly, what you can honestly claim. In business analytics, the most credible analysts are not the ones who found the strongest results. They're the ones who knew exactly where their analysis stopped and said so clearly.*

*In the next 90 minutes, we're looking for four things: a specific question, an appropriate method, an honest result, and a clear statement of limitations. The last one is the hardest to get right — and the most professionally important."*

---

### Group Presentations (45–60 minutes)

Each group has 10 minutes to present, followed by 5 minutes of Q&A.

**Who asks Q&A questions:**
- First 2 minutes: the assigned challenge group from Week 21 (they have context and prepared follow-ups)
- Next 2 minutes: the broader class and instructor
- Last 1 minute: one question from the instructor: *"What would you do differently with more time, more data, or a different model?"*

The instructor's closing question is non-negotiable — it appears in every group's Q&A. Groups should prepare a specific answer, not "we'd collect more data" but "we'd add X variable to the regression to control for Y confound that we identified in Week 21."

**Presentation evaluation (not graded here — but criteria to be clear):**

| Criterion | What it means |
|---|---|
| Question specificity | Answerable, testable, decision-relevant |
| Method appropriateness | Correct choice given the data and question; stated assumptions |
| Result honesty | Reported with uncertainty; does not overstate precision |
| Limitation acknowledgement | At least one specific limitation that constrains the conclusion |
| Response to challenge | Can defend or revise claims under direct questioning |

**Note:** the Week 22 seminar is not the assessed component. The individual case study coursework (30%) is a separate submission. Week 22 is the rehearsal and the capstone experience — the formal milestone at which the course's central argument is demonstrated by students, not taught.

---

### Reflective Close (10–15 minutes)

After all presentations: the instructor leads a brief collective reflection (not a summary — a question):

*"We started Week 1 by asking: what does 'modelling' actually mean, and what does a model give up in order to be useful? After 22 weeks — what's your answer?"*

Students respond briefly. Expected themes:
- A model is a simplified representation that makes a specific question answerable
- What it gives up: detail, context, alternative interpretations, causal claims beyond the data
- What it gains: tractability, comparability, a defensible basis for a decision

The instructor names the thread: from decision-making under uncertainty (Week 1) to final analysis (Week 22), every session has been asking the same question: what can this method tell us, and what does it assume? Students who leave Week 22 with a clear answer to that question have achieved the course's deepest learning objective.

---

### Individual Written Reflection (10 minutes)

Students write individually (not as a group) — submitted on paper or digitally at the end of the session:

1. What is one claim you can make from this course's analysis that you couldn't make at the start of Week 1?
2. What is one question you started this course wanting to answer that you now know is harder than you thought?
3. What is one skill — specific — that you will use in your next internship or job?

These are not graded. They are the individual close of a course that was otherwise largely group-based. They create a personal anchor for the learning, distinct from the group analysis, that students can return to.

---

## After the Course

**LMS final log entry (group, not individual):**

"Over four weeks, our question evolved from ___ to ___. The most important analytical decision we made was ___. The thing we'd do differently is ___. The skill we developed most in Weeks 19–22 is ___."

This is the group's narrative of intellectual growth. It is not assessed but it is required — Forward College's emphasis on reflective practice means the course does not end with a presentation; it ends with a documented account of what changed.

---

## Design Rationale

| Design choice | Pedagogical grounding |
|---|---|
| "What would you do differently?" as non-negotiable Q&A question | Bjork (1994): retrieval and elaboration — a specific, thoughtful answer requires retrieving the analysis, evaluating it against an alternative, and articulating the gap; this is the deepest cognitive task in the session |
| Week 22 is not the graded assessment | The coursework (30%) is a separate individual submission; making Week 22 itself non-graded removes the performance pressure that would otherwise prevent students from acknowledging genuine limitations in their presentations |
| Individual reflection after group presentation | Vygotsky (1978): the group is the scaffold; the individual reflection is the internalization; students who can state their personal learning independently of the group have done the deeper cognitive work |
| Reflective close anchors back to Week 1 | Cepeda et al. (2006): spacing effect — retrieving the Week 1 question ("what does modelling give up?") after 22 weeks and answering it with 22 weeks of experience produces the strongest retention |
| Presentation criteria made explicit (not secret) | Rosenshine (2012): clarity about what success looks like reduces performance anxiety and enables self-evaluation; students who know the four criteria before presenting can check their own work against them |
| Limitation statement as required element | Forward College Year 3: accountability and wise decision-making; an analysis without stated limitations is not a complete professional product; requiring it signals that intellectual honesty is the course's final standard |

---

## Pedagogical Design Challenges

### 1. Some groups will present results without limitations because they think it makes them look weak.

Academic culture sometimes rewards strong-looking results without caveats. The Week 22 standard explicitly inverts this: a presentation without limitations is incomplete.

**Resolution:** the pre-class note to groups missing limitations (see Pre-work section) gives advance warning. The instructor also names it in the opening: "A result with stated limitations is more credible than the same result without them. A doctor who says 'this diagnosis has 85% confidence' is more trustworthy than one who says 'I'm sure.'"

---

### 2. The "what would you do differently?" question may produce generic answers.

"We'd collect more data" or "we'd use a more advanced method" are common deflections. They don't demonstrate genuine analytical reflection.

**Resolution:** the week 21 group log entry asks groups to write "one specific next step that would improve it." The Week 22 Q&A question should follow from that log entry. If a group's log entry said "we'd add neighbourhood income data to the regression," the instructor's Week 22 question can be: "You mentioned adding neighbourhood income. What effect do you expect that would have on your main coefficient, and why?"

---

### 3. The 10-minute presentation format is tight for groups with complex analyses.

Groups who tried to do too much (multiple models, many variables, several hypotheses) will struggle to fit their analysis into 10 minutes.

**Resolution:** the 3–4 sentence narrative from Week 21 is the template. If it fits in 4 sentences, it fits in 10 minutes. Groups who can't summarise their analysis in 4 sentences haven't decided what their claim is — which is a signal that the analysis isn't finished, not that 10 minutes is too short.

---

### 4. Year 3 cohort dynamics may produce an audience that is supportive to the point of being uncritical in Q&A.

Students who have known each other for two years may ask easy questions to support their friends rather than genuine analytical challenges.

**Resolution:** the assigned challenge group from Week 21 has already prepared three specific questions. They go first in Q&A and the bar is already set. The instructor asks the closing question, which is always analytical. Between the challenge group's questions and the instructor's question, there is sufficient critical engagement regardless of the broader cohort's dynamic in the open Q&A period.

---

### 5. The individual reflection at the end may feel anticlimactic after a group presentation.

Students who have just completed a collaborative arc may experience the individual reflection as a step down — returning to individual mode after building something together.

**Resolution:** frame it explicitly: "The group work is done. This 10 minutes is yours. What did you, specifically, take from this course that you didn't have before?" The cohort's shared experience becomes the backdrop against which individual reflection is most meaningful — not despite the group work, but because of it. This is particularly true for a Forward College cohort that has been doing individual reflection work since Lisbon Year 1.

# Supplement (2026-07-06): Textbook Cross-Reference, Extended Materials, Alternative Activities, Critique

## 1. Textbook Cross-Reference — Albright & Winston, 6th ed.

**Appendix A-3 ("Examples of Statistical Reports," pp. A-7–A-18)** is the reference model for what groups are presenting — complete analyses with stated methods, results, and caveats. Circulate one example with the pre-work as the calibration artefact ("your 10 minutes should sound like this reads"). Combined with A-2's clear/concise/precise standards (assigned in Weeks 19–21 per those supplements), the capstone block then has a textbook spine even though it introduces no new methods — which also answers a question an interview panel might ask about these four weeks ("where's the book in your capstone?").

## 2. Extended Materials

**E1 — Q&A question bank (arm the audience; with what strong answers sound like).** Five question archetypes for the open-Q&A segment, so class questions rise above "how did you find the dataset?":

1. *Assumption probe:* "Which single assumption, if wrong, would most change your conclusion?" — Strong answer names one (not "several"), states the direction of the error, and says what was done to check it. Weak: "our data was quite clean."
2. *Alternative-method probe:* "Why this method and not [obvious alternative]?" — Strong: names the trade-off ("regression let us control for size; a t-test on binned groups would discard that"). Weak: "it's the one we knew."
3. *Generalisation probe:* "Would this hold in [other city/year/population]?" — Strong: identifies which feature of the data is local and which mechanism might travel. Weak: unqualified yes.
4. *Decision probe:* "You're advising the responsible manager — what exactly should they do differently on Monday?" — Strong: one concrete action plus the confidence caveat. Weak: "they should look into it further."
5. *Effect-size probe:* "Is the effect big enough to matter, and compared to what?" — Strong: restates the effect in decision units (€, minutes, percentage points) against a benchmark. Weak: re-quotes the p-value.

**E2 — Anchored rubric for the five criteria (the table currently lists criteria without levels).** One line per criterion at three levels, publishable in advance per the plan's own criteria-not-secret philosophy:

| Criterion | Developing | Solid | Exemplary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question specificity | topic, not question | testable question | testable + the decision it informs stated |
| Method appropriateness | method named | method justified vs an alternative | + assumptions checked and shown |
| Result honesty | point estimate only | estimate with uncertainty | + effect size in decision units |
| Limitation | generic ("more data") | specific, named mechanism | + its direction of bias on the conclusion |
| Response to challenge | defends everything | accept/dispute/qualify used | + revises the claim live when warranted |

**E3 — The closing-question follow-up tree.** The non-negotiable question ("what would you do differently…") has a scripted escalation when the answer is generic: generic answer ("more data") → "more data *on what variable*, and what would it change?" → still generic → "your Week 21 log named [X] as the unaddressed limitation — how would you attack that?" The Week 21 log entries are the instructor's ammunition; this is why they exist.

## 3. Alternative In-Class Activities (additional options)

**A. One-slide abstract gallery (5 min, replaces part of the opening).** Every group's Week 21 3–4 sentence narrative goes up as a single slide, side by side, before any presentation. The room sees the four claims at once — and the reflective close ("what does a model give up?") lands harder against four concrete, differently-shaped answers. Also functions as the session's insurance: if tech fails mid-presentation, the abstract slide plus talk survives.

**B. Audience criterion cards (0 min, converts spectating into judging).** Each audience member holds one of the five criteria and scores every presentation on it (E2 anchors). After the final Q&A, one sentence per criterion-holder in the reflective close: "the strongest limitation statement today was ___ because ___." Distributes the evaluative work of the missing external evaluator across the room — and it is itself assessment literacy practice for the individual coursework.

**C. Cold-open Q&A swap (variation).** Reverse the printed Q&A order once per session: instructor's closing question *first* for one group. Groups uniformly script their opening minute and improvise the ending; reversing once tests whether the "what would you do differently" answer is genuinely held or queued. Use on the strongest group (per Week 20–21 evidence), not the weakest.
 
**D. Course-arc retrieval finale (10 min, optional, in the reflective close).** Ten rapid Mentimeter questions spanning the 22 weeks (one per block theme: what a model omits; median vs mean; confound; base rate; distribution choice; CI meaning; p-value meaning; coefficient conditionality; forecast assumption; GIGO). Ungraded, anonymous. It closes the weekly-quiz thread the course itself justified with Farmus et al., gives students a private signal of exam readiness, and ends the course inside its own pedagogy.

**E. Letter to next year's cohort (alternative individual reflection).** Same 10 minutes, same three questions, but addressed to a Week 1 student of next year's course. Same content, warmer register — and it produces an artefact the instructor can actually use next year (with permission), which honours Forward's reflective-practice emphasis beyond ritual.

## 4. Critique of the Lesson Plan

**What works (keep):** the non-negotiable closing question wired to the Week 21 logs (Design Challenge 2's specific-follow-up move is exactly right); the pre-class limitation check with advance warning; not grading the session (correctly reasoned in the rationale); the reflective close reaching back to Week 1's question — the longest arc in the course, deliberately closed; the individual reflection separating personal learning from the group product.

**Problems, reasons, and fixes:**

1. **The session's arithmetic doesn't reconcile with itself.** The structure table gives Opening 5 + Presentations 45–60 + Reflective close 10–15 + Individual reflection 10 (= 70–90), but the adjustment bullets say "3 groups: 15 min per group + 15 min close = 60 min total," silently dropping the opening and the individual reflection. Full schedules: **3 groups** = 5 + 45 + 15 + 10 = 75 min (spare 15 → extend Q&A to 7–8 min per group, as the bullet half-suggests: 5 + 54 + 15 + 10 = 84 ✓); **4 groups** = 5 + 60 + 10 + 10 = 85 ✓. Print those two lines and delete the broken bullets.
2. **The external evaluator promised in Weeks 19, 20, and 21 does not exist in this plan.** This is the terminal file of that dangling thread (Week 21 even ships the unresolved bracket). *Fix:* decide and align all four files. If a guest is invited: add a one-paragraph guest brief (the five criteria + E1's question archetypes + "ask the generalisation probe"), slot their question into the Q&A's middle segment, and warn groups in Week 21. If not: Activity B's criterion cards are the honest substitute — say so in Weeks 19–20.
3. **Criteria without anchors.** The evaluation table defines each criterion in one clause but gives no levels, so groups can't self-locate and the class can't calibrate its Q&A judgements. E2 fixes this at one table's cost — and publishing it *is* the plan's own stated philosophy.
4. **The pre-class submission has no deadline.** "Submit the final presentation before class" gives the instructor no time to run the completeness check and send the missing-limitation note the plan promises. *Fix:* 48 hours before class, with the note sent within 24 — otherwise the safety net is decorative.
5. **The individual reflections go nowhere.** They are collected and never used — yet Forward's weekly one-on-one tutorials (unused across all 22 plans; flagged in the Week 1 supplement) are the natural venue: the final tutorial of term discusses the student's own three answers. One sentence connects the course's ending to the institution's most distinctive teaching structure.
6. **No failure contingencies for a terminal session.** A missing group member, a corrupted notebook, or a projector failure has no fallback in the one week that can't be re-run. *Fix:* require the exported slide PDF *and* the one-slide abstract (Activity A) in the pre-class submission; designate every member able to present any section (state this in Week 21 so groups rehearse for it).
7. **The course ends without pointing at the exam.** ST2187 is ultimately examined by the University of London; Week 22 closes the teaching arc but says nothing about the revision phase. One closing paragraph — "the weekly quiz bank, the tutorial questions T0–T8 across Weeks 1–15, and the answer keys are your revision set; the exam tests exactly the interpretation skills the Q&A just rehearsed" — converts 22 weeks of materials into the revision resource they already are. (Minor: "forward College" → "Forward College" in the After the Course section.)
