# Interview Feedback & Points for Improvement

Synthesised from post-rejection feedback across multiple processes.

---

## 1. Communication: lead with the conclusion, then stop

**The pattern:** Answers run long because thorough preparation surfaces more context than the question required. In fast-paced environments (PM-heavy, high-context cultures), over-explaining reads as uncertainty rather than rigour.

**The fix:**
- Treat the result sentence as the full stop. If they want more, they'll ask.
- Deliver stories at ~70% of the detail that feels "complete."
- Don't fill silence with elaboration — land the point and wait.

*Sources: Seven Senders ("keep answers concise and leading with the conclusion"); Distribusion ("more concise and structured communication, especially when discussing complex topics")*

---

## 2. Ambiguity framing: show functioning in noise, not just converting noise to structure

**The pattern:** Stories about proactive improvement and systematic fixes demonstrate initiative in a *stable* environment. That's not the same as comfort with genuine chaos — competing urgent requests, shifting priorities, no structure to fall back on.

**The fix:**
- Build STAR stories that show *thriving without structure*, not just *creating structure*.
- Specifically: stories where priorities shifted mid-execution, where you had to drop something to pick up something else, and where the outcome was still good.
- Don't just show you fix systems — show you function when there is no system.

*Source: Distribusion ("comfort and effectiveness in situations where priorities shift frequently, multiple urgent requests compete for attention, and ambiguity is a regular part of the day-to-day")*

---

## 3. In-room red flags: name them, don't just note them

**The pattern:** When something surfaces in an interview that signals misalignment — a comment about chaotic backlogs, unclear ownership, high-spend with no guardrails — it gets flagged internally but not probed.

**The fix:**
- When a red flag appears, ask about it directly. "That's interesting — how does the team typically handle that?" is both a genuine probe and a signal that you operate proactively.
- Not naming it reads as passive. Naming it reads as exactly the behaviour high-ambiguity roles want.

*Source: Distribusion (Kilian interview — red flag noticed but not acted on)*

---

## 4. Culture fit: interrogate the operating environment before final rounds

**The pattern:** Technical preparation is thorough. The gap is not reading the operating culture early enough to calibrate or self-select out.

**Questions to ask explicitly before or during final rounds:**
- "How are priorities set week to week, and how often do they change?"
- "What does a typical Monday look like for this role?"
- "How does the team handle competing urgent requests?"

Use the answer to calibrate fit, not just to appear curious.

*Source: Distribusion (three interviewers had three different profiles in mind; cultural misalignment with Russian Big Tech operating style)*

---

## 5. dbt depth (role-specific gap)

**The pattern:** BI and analytics engineering foundation is strong, but hands-on dbt depth has been a tipping point in at least one process.

**The fix:** When applying to roles where dbt is listed as a core requirement, be explicit about the depth of hands-on experience (models authored, testing patterns used, macro or package usage). If the depth is thin, name it early and reframe around learning speed.

*Source: Seven Senders ("the other candidate had more hands-on depth with dbt specifically, which was the tipping point")*
