## The arc

I started with a hunger to understand how systems work — not just superficially, but deeply enough to improve them. Early on, I gravitated toward roles where I could see direct impact: building things that mattered, fixing processes that were broken, learning expertise in domains most people avoided. 

The consistent thread has been seeking out hard problems and the autonomy to solve them properly. I've always been someone who notices what's broken — and can't leave it alone until it's fixed. That drive has shaped my choices: toward technical depth over breadth, toward clarity over consensus, toward merit-based achievement over politics. I've learned that I do my best work when I have clear goals, space to execute independently, and the trust of people who respect directness.

But it goes deeper than just solving for myself. I'm genuinely driven to help others — not through grand gestures or constant availability, but through quiet competence and reliability. I want to be the person people can count on, who creates clarity where there's confusion, who gives honest feedback as an investment in someone's growth. I care about fairness and doing right by people, and I'm energised by knowing my work makes a tangible difference in someone else's ability to succeed.

At each step, I've looked for environments where engineering and logic matter — where I'm judged on results, not relationships. I've built expertise in [domains], become a go-to resource on problems others find intractable, and pulled teams up to higher standards. But I've also learned that I work best in small-to-medium teams with high bars, not sprawling organizations, and that the people matter as much as the problem.

As I've progressed, I've noticed something: I care about more than just solving the problem myself. I want to help others get better at solving problems too. I believe that good work — and good leadership — comes from understanding what motivates and strengths people have, setting them up to play to those strengths, and giving them the room to grow at their own pace. I'm drawn to the idea of servant leadership: using expertise to help others improve, advocating for their accomplishments, and creating the conditions where they can focus on what actually matters. But I also know this isn't natural for me — I'm more comfortable with logic than with emotions, and I have to be intentional about seeing the emotional dimension of how my directness lands on others.

Now I'm looking to apply that pattern at a larger scale: deep expertise in a high-impact domain, autonomy to shape how problems get solved, and collaborators who value the same things I do — truth, competence, and results. If I move into leadership, it will be to amplify that impact — not to become someone I'm not, but to be genuinely useful to people who work with me.

## The one thread

I'm someone who gets energized by solving hard problems well, and I structure my life around maximising the time I spend on actual work and minimising time spent on politics or consensus-building.
