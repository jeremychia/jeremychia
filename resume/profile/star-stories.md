# STAR Stories

Pre-written stories organised by theme. Use the STAR+Spark structure: **Situation/Task** (20%) → **Action** using "I" not "we" (60%) → **Result** with a number (20%) → **Learning/Spark** (one sentence).

Add stories as you remember them. Aim for at least one strong story per theme.

## Leadership / Influencing without authority

### Story 1: Works Council Election (Berlin Office)
*Feedback from: Azéline Boucher (colleague in Works Council)*

**Situation/Task:**
Vinted Berlin office needed someone to organize and execute the works council election — a legally complex, high-stakes process with no clear playbook. I was asked to lead it despite it being outside my typical technical role.

**Action:**
I treated it like a technical project: broke down legal requirements into clear milestones, researched and documented each step, coordinated stakeholders (HR, employees, legal), and designed the process to be transparent and inclusive at each phase. I kept everyone informed, managed expectations clearly, and stayed deeply involved in execution to ensure nothing fell through cracks. I emphasized the "why" — not just getting it done, but modeling that this was a care issue, not a box-to-tick.

**Result:**
Election ran smoothly with full participation; stakeholders explicitly praised the organization, leadership, and genuine inclusivity. One senior stakeholder noted: "Your leadership, time management, project management, and stakeholder management were all top-notch. You made a complicated process feel smooth and well-coordinated." The process became a model for other offices.

**Learning:**
I learned that leadership isn't about title — it's about clarity, ownership, and making people feel like their participation matters. My technical precision and directness, applied to people work, creates trust. I also learned I can care deeply about non-technical work when the principles are the same: clear expectations, systematic execution, honest communication.

## Cross-functional collaboration

### Story 1: P/L Dashboard & FinOps Partnership
*Feedback from: Monika Milaknytė (Pricing), Samanta Vasilenkaitė (Finance), Vytautė Pilkytė (FinOps)*

**Situation/Task:**
The Pricing team needed a P/L dashboard to understand financial impact of their decisions. But the real challenge was deeper: Finance and FinOps teams worked in silos, neither fully understood the other's constraints, and data quality issues cascaded between them.

**Action:**
Instead of just building the dashboard, I invested time understanding both teams' worlds — their definitions, workflows, constraints. I proposed a dashboard but structured it to help teams see data issues themselves and understand the *why* behind financial decisions. I documented processes, organized joint workshops, and in code reviews, I explained not just what was wrong but why it mattered for the domain. I became a translator: helping Finance understand FinOps constraints and vice versa.

**Result:**
Dashboard became a crucial tool for Pricing (Monika: "crucial tool for the pricing team...I am very grateful for your help"). But the bigger win was team alignment and self-sufficiency. Samanta noted my "forward-thinking mindset" and how I "proposed solutions and improvements instead of just pointing out problems." FinOps team (Vytautė) praised my ability to "get into the details of every issue" and "explain everything in detail, not just to give a plain answer."

**Learning:**
The best technical solutions fail if they don't account for the human/organizational context. My willingness to understand other domains and help teams improve — not just deliver — creates trust and enables them to grow beyond my help.

## Driving change or process improvement

### Story 1: Data Catalog Ownership & DWH Quality Standards
*Feedback from: Gert Lõhmus (Data Platform lead), Junn Kim (peer engineer), Inga Šneiderytė (Data Infrastructure)*

**Situation/Task:**
Data Catalog initiative was stalled: no clear ownership, no shared accountability for which team owned which data asset, and no mechanism to ensure quality at scale. Separately, I noticed data quality issues cascading through the DWH that required systematic improvements.

**Action:**
I took on both challenges in parallel. For Data Catalog, I drove the shift from "theoretically clear" ownership to *real* accountability — working with teams to understand what ownership meant, then auditing and correcting every data asset. For DWH quality, I applied the same rigor: reviewed PRs systematically, pushed for better tests and documentation, and worked with engineers across teams (outside my domain) to improve standards. I did this without formal authority — by demonstrating value and building trust.

**Result:**
Data Catalog became a functioning tool. Gert noted: "Your proactive approach in partnering with and becoming the delivery team...was needed. You brought us the user insights, you challenged us." Code quality improved measurably; Junn praised my "thorough code reviews" and attention to data quality across domains. Inga described me as "a true role model in co-creation, proactivity, and eagerness to learn and help." I became a recognized quality voice even in teams that weren't mine and influenced the guild's standards upward.

**Learning:**
Real change comes from clarity about what matters and relentless follow-through. It also requires meeting people where they are — I had to understand what "ownership" meant to different teams and help them see why standards mattered to them, not just to me. Authority comes from demonstrated judgment, not title.

## Mentoring or growing others

### Story 1: VIntern Sustainability Project
*Feedback from: Karl Healy (intern mentee), Goda Lesevičiūtė (mentor coordinator)*

**Situation/Task:**
Mentored a team of 3-4 interns on a self-directed sustainability project with ambiguous scope and unclear deliverables. The goal was real impact, but the team felt uncertain about what "done" looked like.

**Action:**
I gave the team room to explore and develop their own ideas, but structured the mentorship around clarity: helped them understand project scope, reviewed their thinking critically (not just supportively), and asked questions that forced them to think through assumptions. I was invested and present in discussions, but didn't prescribe solutions — I let them own the direction while helping them avoid obvious pitfalls. I created a team dynamic where honest feedback was normal and expected.

**Result:**
Team completed the project with clear deliverables and developed strong relationships with each other. Karl noted: "You're super knowledgeable and well connected...You gave us room to come up with our own ideas and your level of input was perfect." Goda observed: "You helped create a constructive group dynamic where the group was comfortable sharing honest opinions...You were very caring of your intern project team."

**Learning:**
Good mentoring isn't about being warm or always available — it's about creating clarity and space for people to do their best thinking. People feel cared for when they're held to a high standard with clear feedback, not when everything is easy. My directness and high standards, paired with genuine investment in their success, creates the conditions where people grow.
