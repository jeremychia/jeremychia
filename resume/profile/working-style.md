# Working Style

## Team dynamics
- Best in teams of 5–15: large enough for interesting problems, small enough to know everyone and maintain shared standards
- Natural role: problem solver + standard-setter + quiet supporter; not the social connector
- Works best with 1–2 peers to debate with — collaborative on hard problems, not consensus-seeking
- Shows care through reliability and honest feedback, not constant warmth or availability

## Handling ambiguity
- Uncomfortable initially → immediately tries to narrow it; asks clarifying questions, proposes structure, locks down constraints with stakeholders
- Needs some guardrails: total open-endedness is paralyzing; needs defined problem space even if solution is undefined
- May seem impatient with exploration — prefers "here's what I think we should test" over open brainstorming

## Giving and receiving feedback
- **Receiving:** direct, logical, specific; "that approach won't work because..." is useful; don't soften it
- **Giving:** direct and fact-based; assumes people want to improve; may not realise delivery lands as harsh
- **Blindspot:** can miss that others need encouragement alongside correction; working on feedback that opens doors, not closes them — especially with self-critical people

## Under pressure
- Moderate pressure: focuses tighter, moves faster — stress clarifies thinking and prioritisation
- Extreme pressure: brief withdrawal to reset, then reengage; quiet ≠ giving up
- Needs: clear escalation paths, autonomy to decide fast, minimal process overhead in a crisis; needs downtime afterward to recharge

## Working with stakeholders
- Default: direct and efficient; tells stakeholders what I think and why; won't pretend a bad solution is good
- Can find unconventional solutions as a pragmatist; will explore alternatives if constraints are real
- Drains over time — needs technical work and solitude to recharge after intensive stakeholder periods
- Learning: people decide based on context I may not see; find where interests align before dismissing; try to understand what problem they're solving first

## As a mentor
- Teaches frameworks and reasoning, not just tools — stays relevant as technology changes
- Co-creates with mentees; uses their context and goals to make material relevant, not a one-size prescription
- Adapts to motivation profiles: some want operational expertise, some management, some collaboration skills — helps them identify which path fits them
- Stays connected after the engagement; learns from how people apply what they learned

## As a leader
- Sets crisp expectations and makes trade-offs explicit; teams know where they stand
- Advocates fiercely for team accomplishments; won't let good work go unrecognised
- Stays hands-on in domain — leads through technical judgment, not pure delegation
- Matches people to problems based on what actually motivates them (mastery / impact / autonomy)
- Protects focus time; actively removes obstacles: meetings, vague roadmaps, politics
- **Watch for:** directness landing as harsh without context; lack of check-ins read as indifference (be explicit it means trust); impatience with others' pace
